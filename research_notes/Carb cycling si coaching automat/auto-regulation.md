# Auto-regulating a nutrition plan from measured data (scale weight, intake logs, subjective ratings)

Scope note: the target is a single-user phone app with daily morning weights, daily logged intake, training-day flags, and 1-10 ratings (hunger, energy, sleep). No wearables, no DEXA. Findings below are restricted to what can be computed from those inputs.

Important source-quality warning up front: the commercial apps that do this best (MacroFactor, Carbon, RP, Avatar) publish **methodology descriptions but not formulas**. MacroFactor explicitly calls its V3 techniques "secret sauce" and withholds the smoothing constants, lookback length and update equations ([MacroFactor V3 write-up](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)). Carbon describes Coach Trend Weight only as "a proprietary algorithm that combines several statistical techniques" ([Carbon help](https://help.joincarbon.com/en/articles/6078877-coach-trend-weight)). So the implementable numbers below come from (a) the open Hacker's Diet EWMA, (b) peer-reviewed energy-balance models, (c) the behavioural/qualitative thresholds the apps *do* publish, and (d) Stronger By Science's open diet guide. Anything presented as a complete step-by-step algorithm from Helms, Norton or RP could **not** be sourced and is flagged as a gap.

## Weight-trend smoothing: EWMA vs simple averages, and how long before a trend change is real

### Takeaway
The only fully published, reproducible smoothing algorithm is John Walker's Hacker's Diet EWMA with smoothing constant S = 0.9 (i.e. alpha = 0.1), which lags roughly like a 20-day simple moving average; modern apps use longer-timescale, recency-weighted averages whose constants are undisclosed, and the practical consensus is that a deviation needs to persist ~4-5 days to be worth reacting to at all and 3-4 weeks to be treated as a confirmed change in expenditure.

### Cited Findings
- Hacker's Diet trend: `trend_d = trend_{d-1} + (1 - S) * (weight_d - trend_{d-1})` with **S = 0.9**, so the daily update applies 0.1 of the gap between today's weight and yesterday's trend — "with the smoothing constant S=0.9 we use on weight data, we calculate the new trend value Ad from the previous trend value Ad-1 and today's weight Md" — [Fourmilab / The Hacker's Diet](https://fourmilab.ch/hackdiet/www/subsubsection1_2_4_0_4_3.html)
- The 0.1 is the complement P = 1 - S, which Walker calls the "smoothing percentage"; "10% smoothing" therefore means S = 0.9. Walker explicitly warns the two forms are easy to confuse — [Fourmilab](https://fourmilab.ch/hackdiet/www/subsubsection1_2_4_0_4_3.html)
- S = 0.9 is "roughly equivalent to a 20 day simple moving average in terms of lagging the trend" — [Fourmilab](https://fourmilab.ch/hackdiet/www/subsubsection1_4_1_0_8_3.html)
- Smoothing constants below 1 weight recent data more heavily, and the recency bias grows as S falls toward zero — [Fourmilab](https://fourmilab.ch/hackdiet/www/subsubsection1_2_4_0_4_4.html)
- One user comparing the Hacker's Diet average against their own gapped data found it reacts to real weight changes **more slowly than theory predicts** (ratio of c² to σ² "nowhere near 1/90 — it was actually about 1/5"). Anecdotal forum comment, not a formal analysis — [Beeminder forum / GreaterWrong comment](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/6oYETaG248zGF45aD/anti-akrasia-tool-like-stickk-com-for-data-nerds/comment/wPAvsXXaG46rjc7nS)
- MacroFactor's trend weight uses "a long-timescale averaging approach that weights recent values more heavily" (exact parameters not given); short deviations are largely ignored, and "a deviation that persists to about day four or five, especially if accelerating, triggers adjustment" — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- MacroFactor's expenditure estimate "uses roughly three weeks of data," so short gaps inside a single week don't meaningfully move it — [MacroFactor help: weigh-in frequency](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/109-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-weight-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates)
- A stall or surge "persisting for 3-4 weeks gives MacroFactor much more confidence in a real expenditure change" — [MacroFactor help: adjustments](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)
- Responsiveness/stability trade-off stated explicitly: "a one-year window is very stable but slow, and a one-week window is responsive but noisy"; shorter lookbacks are more responsive but noisier — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/); [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- V3 vs V2 quantified: trend reversals detected **1-5 days sooner** (~3 days in the author's own data), and **day-to-day expenditure updates ~35% smaller** — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- Carbon recomputes trend values for **every past day** whenever a new weigh-in is added, so historical trend values change retroactively (a backward-smoothing / state-space approach, not a causal EWMA) — [Carbon help](https://help.joincarbon.com/en/articles/6078877-coach-trend-weight)
- A smart-scale study found that "body weight imputation using structural modeling with Kalman smoothing or an exponentially weighted moving average provided the best agreement with observed values," and that "simple linear approaches to the measurement of BWV (such as RMSE around the linear trend) are not able to fully differentiate the overall trend from the variability component" — [JMIR mHealth 2020;8(9):e17977](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/9/e17977)
- Magnitude of the irreducible daily noise: a three-year personal dataset fit with a smooth trend reported "final RMSE of ~0.85 kg is the irreducible day-to-day scatter: meal timing, hydration, measurement noise." Personal-blog analysis, not peer-reviewed — [Three Years of Daily Weight (via gwern mirror)](https://gwern.net/doc/www/jbogomolovas2.github.io/81f9e85167f5fa9573b4a8c74563b439e05e0468.html) (note: the page body did not load on fetch; the figure comes from the search-result excerpt, so treat it as low-confidence)
- A weight-management patent states "the typical weight fluctuation associated with water gain and loss can be three or more pounds per day"; a practitioner blog describing a weight-stable person reports readings within ±1.25 lb of their average. These conflict — likely different people/conditions — [US patent 11633160](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11633160); [LeanBlog](https://www.leanblog.org/2019/03/weigh-yourself-every-day-without-freaking-out/)

### Noise sources, with magnitudes (needed to size the smoothing window)
- 1 L of fluid ≈ 1 kg of scale weight — [Sigma Nutrition, The Scale Paradox](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Each 1 g of stored glycogen holds ~3 g of water. Muscle stores ~400-800 g carbohydrate, liver ~80-100 g. Worked example: +400 g muscle glycogen → +1.2 kg water → **+1.6 kg total scale weight** — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- A single large overfeed day moves the scale **0.5-1 kg (1-2 lb) within 24 h**, almost none of it fat — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Creatine: loading plus ~5 g/day raised total body water **6.2% over 4 weeks** in one study; ~1.1 kg water gain over 42 days — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Sodium: high-salt meals add acute water weight that "typically reverses after a couple of days of solid dietary adherence" — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Menstrual cycle: some women gain roughly **0.45-4.54 kg (1-10 lb)** in certain phases; the recommendation is to compare rolling averages **month-to-month at the same cycle phase** — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Low-carb onset: rapid early loss that is mostly water and glycogen, "typically within about the first week," and it rebounds when carbs return — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/); [Stronger By Science diet guide](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- MacroFactor names the specific false-signal set it had to dampen in V3: short-term water shifts from glycogen, food volume, carb and salt intake, creatine, and the menstrual cycle — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- MacroFactor states that a persistent non-energy weight change (starting creatine, or a large shift in carbs or fibre) causes **roughly one to two weeks of inappropriate adjustments**, and a week-long activity spike takes **about two weeks to wash out** — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- Practical protocol for reducing measurement variance: weigh daily, first thing in the morning, after urinating, minimal clothing, before eating or drinking; use **weekly or bi-weekly rolling averages** rather than isolated readings; never use a single weigh-in once per week — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/); same standardisation given in [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)

### Inferences
- With daily noise SD on the order of 0.5-1.0 kg and a target signal of ~0.5-1.0 kg/week, the signal-to-noise ratio per day is roughly 1:5 to 1:10. Detecting a *change in rate* therefore needs on the order of 2-4 weeks of data — which is exactly the window MacroFactor says gives it confidence (3-4 weeks) and exactly why Hacker's Diet picked a ~20-day-equivalent lag. This convergence from two independent directions is the strongest quantitative anchor in this research.
- The EWMA at alpha = 0.1 is the right default for a single-user app: it needs only one stored state value per day, degrades gracefully with missing days (hold the trend, or skip the update), and its lag is already matched to the noise. Smaller alpha (0.05, ~40-day equivalent) would be too slow for a 2-3 week check-in cadence; larger alpha (0.2-0.3) reintroduces water noise into the adjustment decision.
- A clean, defensible two-layer design: layer 1 = EWMA(alpha ≈ 0.1) for *display* and for the user's day-to-day emotional regulation; layer 2 = a *rate* estimate (slope) computed over a longer window, e.g. ordinary least squares on the last 14-21 daily weights, or the difference of two EWMA values 14 days apart divided by 14. Only layer 2 should drive calorie changes. This separates "what do I weigh" from "how fast am I moving," which is the quantity the adjustment rule actually needs.
- Carbon's retroactive recomputation implies a *smoother* (two-sided, e.g. Kalman smoother or LOESS) rather than a *filter*. A smoother is more accurate for historical display but cannot be used for today's decision, because today's point is always at the edge where a two-sided method has no future data. For an app, use a filter for decisions and optionally a smoother for the chart.
- The menstrual-cycle magnitude (up to 4.5 kg) exceeds any plausible weekly fat change, so for a menstruating user an EWMA alone is insufficient; comparison must be phase-matched (28-day-lagged comparison) or the adjustment interval extended to a full cycle. Not applicable to this project's single male user, but worth noting if the app generalises.

### Gaps
- No source gives the actual smoothing constant, half-life, or lookback length used by MacroFactor, Carbon, Happy Scale, or Libra. Happy Scale and Libra specifically returned no methodology documentation in any search — I found no reliable source on their internals. Their reputation as "EWMA apps" is widely assumed but I could not verify the constant.
- No peer-reviewed study reports a population standard deviation for within-person day-to-day weight noise. The 0.85 kg RMSE figure is from a single personal dataset and the page did not fully load; the ±1.25 lb and "3+ lb" figures conflict and are from non-scientific sources. **The app should estimate this per-user from residuals rather than hard-code a constant.**
- No source gives a formal statistical power calculation for "days needed to detect a trend change of X kg/week at given noise." This has to be derived, not cited.

## Published adjustment rules: when and how much to change calories

### Takeaway
Published frameworks agree on the architecture — set a target rate as a % of bodyweight per week, review weekly or bi-weekly, and change intake only when the trend disagrees with the target — but the specific step sizes and thresholds attributed to Helms, Norton and RP could **not** be verified from primary sources; the only concretely documented decision rule is MacroFactor's, which adjusts intake based on *estimated expenditure*, not on deviation from the goal rate.

### Cited Findings — rate targets and deficit sizes (Stronger By Science, open and detailed)
- Rate of loss: slow-to-moderate **0.25-1.0% bodyweight/week**; aggressive **>1.0%/week**. Rate of gain: slow-to-moderate **0.1-0.25%/week**; aggressive **>0.25%/week** — [Stronger By Science diet guide](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- Deficit as a multiplier of estimated maintenance: conservative cut **0.8-0.9×** (10-20% deficit); aggressive cut **0.6-0.7×** (30-40% deficit). Surplus: slow gain 1.05-1.1×, typical 1.1-1.2× — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- Flat-kcal equivalents (the article says percentages are preferred over flat numbers): deficits of **250-500 kcal** (conservative), **500-750** (moderate), **750-1000** (aggressive); surpluses ~125 (slow), 250-375 (moderate), 500 (aggressive) — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- "Assume" starting point in kcal/lb bodyweight: aggressive cut 11, moderate cut 13, maintenance 15 (most people 14-16), moderate gain 17, aggressive gain 19 — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- Estimating maintenance from observed data "takes a couple of weeks" of daily morning weights plus daily intake logging — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- Order of operations when cutting calories: reduce fat and carbs together until the absolute fat floor is reached, then reduce carbs only. When adding calories: add carbs, fat, or both — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- Explicit instruction not to react to noise: "don't react to day-to-day noise from sodium, carbs, glycogen, gut contents, and hydration"; monitor "in a systematic and nuanced manner" — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)

### Cited Findings — MacroFactor's documented decision rule
- **Trigger is expenditure, not goal deviation.** "Intake recommendations don't change just because weight is moving slower or faster than the goal." If estimated expenditure rises, intake recommendations rise at the next check-in; if it falls, they fall — [MacroFactor help: adjustments](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)
- **No minimum change threshold and no fixed step size:** "we think it's simplest to just recommend adjustments of the magnitude your data calls for" — even changes under 10 kcal are applied — [MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)
- **Damping instead of caps:** a smoothing layer limits overreaction. Worked example: a 500 kcal implied drop in expenditure over three weeks yields only a **200-300 kcal** reduction in the recommendation — i.e. roughly 40-60% of the implied change is passed through — [MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)
- **Interval:** adjustments occur at weekly check-ins, based on actual intake and weight change. Each week is treated as self-contained — overeating in week 1 is **not** compensated in week 2 — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- **Adherence-neutral:** the algorithm does not require you to hit targets in order to update them — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- **Minimum data for an update:** nutrition logged **at least 4 of every 7 days** (7 ideal); weight logged **at least once per 7 days** (3+ ideal) — [MacroFactor help: adjustments](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal). The expenditure-algorithm article gives the weight minimum as "once per week is the minimum, though daily is preferred" — [MacroFactor help: weigh-in frequency](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/109-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-weight-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates)
- **Missing-data behaviour:** V2 required ~80-85% nutrition completeness and paused updates at ≥2 missing days per 7; **V3 pauses only at >3 missing days per 7**. With weight, a single missing entry flips the status from "updating" to "holding"; one new entry restarts it and the algorithm catches up immediately — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/); [MacroFactor help: weigh-in frequency](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/109-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-weight-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates)
- **Documented failure mode:** at the start of a deficit or surplus the app initially overreacts to early (water-driven) weight change, then eases targets back toward the goal rate over **one to two weeks** — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- **Recommended override:** when a known confound is active (activity spike, creatine start), the suggested fix is to **skip check-ins** until the estimate stabilises — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- **Macro allocation on adjustment:** protein scales with estimated lean mass and is adjusted separately; fat and carbs absorb most of the energy change; fat will not drop below a minimum. At very low calories the priority order is **fat protected first, then protein, then carbs** — [MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal); [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)

### Cited Findings — other apps' published thresholds
- RP Diet Coach: "every week, review your weight loss progress and the app will recommend an updated meal & macro plan for the next week." The developer says the algorithm came from "years of diet planning and nutrition coaching data" but gives no technical detail. The app rounds food weights to 5 g of a given macro. HealthKit sync for weigh-ins — [RP Diet Coach App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/rp-diet-coach/id1330041267)
- Avatar Nutrition publishes a dedicated help article on how to measure bodyweight for check-ins and another on how selecting "menstrual cycle" affects adjustments, implying cycle-phase-aware adjustment logic — [Avatar Nutrition: measuring bodyweight](https://avatarnutrition.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/19000075321-how-should-i-measure-my-body-weight-for-check-ins-); [Avatar Nutrition: menstrual cycle](https://avatarnutrition.freshdesk.com/solution/articles/19000075325-how-will-selecting-menstrual-cycle-affect-my-adjustments-)
- One coaching framework (secondary source, self-described as rules of thumb rather than measured results) gives: **hold** calories when the weekly average moved only 0.5-1% of bodyweight; **don't cut** after a poor-sleep or high-stress week; size a cut at roughly **5-8% of intake** — [Coachway: adjusting calories after two weeks](https://coachway.io/articles/how-to-adjust-a-clients-calories-after-two-weeks/)
- Another tool states the rule "change calories only when weight trend and intake point the same direction" — [fitnessvolt check-in tool](https://fitnessvolt.com/tdee-calculator/check-in/)
- A separate app requires **at least 3 of the past 7 days** with both weight and meals logged before it will recalculate calories; another advises **at least 6 days of complete calorie data** before averaging intake — [fitnessvolt](https://fitnessvolt.com/tdee-calculator/check-in/)

### Cited Findings — Norton / reverse dieting
- Norton's own article states that "only a small caloric adjustment is necessary to re-start weight loss," which leaves more metabolic room for a future plateau — [BioLayne: metabolic adaptation & reverse dieting part 2](https://biolayne.com/articles/contest-prep/metabolic-adaptation-reverse-dieting-part-2/)
- Norton notes weight loss sometimes continues early in a reverse diet, while insisting thermodynamics still hold — [BioLayne part 1](https://biolayne.com/articles/contest-prep/metabolic-adaptation-reverse-dieting-part-1/)
- One illustrative reverse-diet pace from a dietetics publication (not Norton): from 1,200 kcal, add **~50 kcal every few weeks** until intake holds weight; the process should take roughly as long as the original weight loss — [Today's Dietitian](https://www.todaysdietitian.com/ask-the-expert-reverse-dieting/)

### Inferences
- The two architectures are genuinely different and the choice matters for implementation. **Error-correcting (Helms/RP/coach style):** compare observed rate to target rate and nudge calories to close the gap. **Model-based (MacroFactor style):** maintain a running TDEE estimate, then set intake = TDEE − (goal rate × energy density). The model-based version is strictly better behaved because it doesn't double-count: if you lose faster than planned *because* your TDEE is higher than thought, error-correction *raises* calories (correct), but if you lose faster because you under-ate, error-correction also raises calories (wrong, it rewards under-eating). MacroFactor's "adherence-neutral" framing is the explicit statement of this: the estimate updates from the data regardless of adherence, and intake is then re-derived.
- The damping ratio is the single most useful number recovered: pass through roughly **half** of the implied change (200-300 of 500 kcal). This is equivalent to applying an EWMA with alpha ≈ 0.5 at the weekly level to the *recommendation*, on top of the smoothing already in the trend. A reasonable implementable default: `new_target = old_target + 0.5 * (model_implied_target - old_target)`.
- "Change calories only when weight trend and intake point the same direction" is a cheap and robust gate that needs nothing but the two series already logged: if the trend stalled *and* logged intake was at target, the deficit is genuinely too small; if the trend stalled *and* logged intake ran over, the fix is adherence, not a smaller target. For a single-user app this prevents the most common failure — ratcheting calories down to punish a logging lapse.
- Because deficits should be set as a **% of bodyweight**, the absolute kcal deficit shrinks automatically during weight loss, which is itself a form of auto-regulation and partially removes the need for discretionary cuts. MacroFactor states this explicitly.
- The Coachway "hold if the weekly average moved 0.5-1%" rule is internally inconsistent with a 0.5-1%/week *target* (it would hold forever on a successful diet) and comes from a self-flagged rule-of-thumb source. I would not implement it. It is reported here only because it was the single explicit numeric hold-band found anywhere.

### Gaps
- **Eric Helms' actual check-in protocol could not be sourced.** Searches returned podcast pages, Goodreads/Scribd listings and third-party write-ups. One site presenting a "Helms-inspired" audit explicitly disclaims that its numeric prompts "are our coaching defaults, not figures from the video." The percentages and intervals in *The Muscle and Strength Pyramid: Nutrition* and the 3DMJ material would have to be read from the book directly. **Do not attribute any specific number to Helms on the basis of this research.**
- **Layne Norton's Fat Loss Forever "ladder" could not be sourced.** No step sizes, check-in timing, or stopping criteria from the book appeared in any search result. The 50 kcal / few weeks figure is from Today's Dietitian, not Norton.
- **RP's auto-regulation internals are undocumented publicly.** Only the App Store listing's "weekly review" claim was findable. No source states RP's trend method, thresholds, or adjustment magnitudes.
- Carbon Diet Coach's trend-to-calorie mapping is explicitly not published; their help article says to contact support.
- No source gives a **minimum interval between changes** as an explicit rule. MacroFactor adjusts weekly; the 3-4 week confidence window is about *expenditure confidence*, not a lockout period. The absence of a published minimum interval is itself notable.

## Dynamic energy expenditure estimation from intake + weight change

### Takeaway
All of these apps rearrange energy balance to solve for expenditure (TDEE = intake − ΔstoredEnergy / Δt), and the published error characterisation is substantial: MacroFactor reports median 100-day expenditure error of ~89-108 kcal/day, needs 14-30 days to calibrate, and documents a structural ~80 kcal/day bias that came purely from asymmetric energy-density assumptions.

### Cited Findings — the core equation and its constants
- The method is a rearrangement: intake minus change in stored energy gives calories out. "Maintaining weight at 2500 kcal/day implies roughly 2500 kcal/day of expenditure" — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- Energy densities used: **fat ≈ 39.5 MJ/kg (≈4,282 kcal/lb)**, **lean tissue ≈ 7.6 MJ/kg (≈824 kcal/lb)**. The familiar 3,500 kcal/lb rule implicitly assumes the lost tissue is **~78% fat** — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- Structural bias found and fixed: V1/V2 assumed a smaller surplus per pound gained than deficit per pound lost. In a simulation with ±1 lb weight noise this produced a **persistent overestimate of ~80 kcal/day**. V3 uses mirrored assumptions; the fix changes estimates by **0-130 kcal/day** for most users — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- Calibration time: the starting estimate holds a few days, begins moving after about a week, and typically stabilises after **roughly 14 to 30 days**, depending on logging consistency and how good the starting estimate was — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- Starting estimate source: custom BMR equations plus custom activity multipliers that separate daily-life activity from purposeful exercise — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- Update mechanism: expenditure is adjusted by **prediction errors** — lose faster or gain slower than predicted and the estimate rises; the reverse lowers it — [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- Magnitude of daily updates in steady state: average daily expenditure change over 100 days was **7.25 kcal/day** (V3) and 7.49 kcal/day (V3 + both modifiers) — [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- Error magnitude: **median 100-day expenditure error fell from ~108 kcal to ~89 kcal** with the modifiers enabled — [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- V3 vs V2 accuracy: ~10% more accurate; weekly absolute errors in predicted weight change ~15% smaller, monthly ~5% smaller — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- Documented error-propagation path: "expenditure is inferred from weight change versus intake, so errors in weight change feed directly into expenditure updates." The first 30 days carry much of the total error, and larger updates are expected during calibration — [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- A measured bias: month-1 weight-change error correlated with the target rate of change (**r = 0.27, p < 0.00001**) — aggressive goals produced systematically worse month-1 predictions. The correction removed this bias — [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- The correction applied: the initial estimate is reduced by **4× the intended weekly rate of loss as a % of bodyweight** (1%/week goal → 4% lower estimate; 2%/week → 8% lower). Combined with a BMR reduction, total initial reductions are **~10% (8.8-11.7%) for 1%/week** and **~15% (12.6-15.4%) for 2%/week** — for a 2,500 kcal TDEE, 10% ≈ 250 kcal — [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- Adaptive thermogenesis: the BMR equation assumes **BMR is 5-8% lower during weight loss**, carried through to total expenditure. Controlled studies cited show total energy expenditure **10-15% lower** than standard TDEE equations predict, beyond changes in fat and lean mass — [MacroFactor expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- Imputation of unlogged intake: V3 estimates intake for unlogged days; those estimates average **~13% error, with over 90% of errors below 30%** — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- Robustness test: with **50% of days randomly deleted**, V3 stayed close to the full-data result, though it was somewhat more conservative during fast expenditure changes — [MacroFactor V3](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/)
- Handling of long gaps: after a gap the app carries forward the last high-confidence expenditure value. Up to **14-30 days of past data** can be back-filled, after which a new program should be created so targets reflect the updated estimate — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)

### Cited Findings — why the static 3,500 kcal rule fails (peer-reviewed)
- The 3,500 kcal/lb rule was derived from the energy content of lost weight and "ignores dynamic physiological adaptations to changes in intake and activity" — [Hall & Chow, Int J Obes 2013](https://www.doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2013.51)
- In a comparison of predictions with actual results, "the majority of subjects exhibited substantially less weight loss than the amount predicted by the 3500 kcal rule" — [PMC3880593](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880593)
- Hall's dynamic model instead predicts an asymptotic plateau: in one worked example, "a plateau with energy equilibrium is predicted by the dynamic model to be reached at **1.4 yrs**" — [PMC3880593](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880593)
- Hall & Chow's key caveat: the core error is ignoring the dynamics of energy balance, **not** the number 3,500 itself — "using a 'corrected' numerical value for the energy content of lost tissue does not repair the 3500-kcal rule without also accounting for the ΔEB dynamics" — [Hall & Chow 2013](https://www.doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2013.51)
- Hall's validated dynamic model is distributed as downloadable Excel and Java applications — [PMC4024447](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4024447)

### Inferences
- The back-calculation is observationally equivalent to a **state estimation problem**: the hidden state is TDEE (slowly drifting), the observations are noisy weight and noisy intake, and the process model is energy balance. A 1-D Kalman filter on TDEE is the natural minimal implementation: process noise set so TDEE can drift ~5-10 kcal/day (matching MacroFactor's observed 7.25 kcal/day average update), measurement noise derived from the user's own weight residual SD. This reproduces the documented behaviour — slow early convergence, ~7 kcal/day steady-state updates, automatic damping — without needing the undisclosed proprietary method.
- Under-reporting is **structurally invisible** to this method and no source claims to correct it algorithmically. MacroFactor's position is behavioural, not statistical: removing penalties for overeating encourages more complete logging. The consequence for implementation is important — a user who chronically under-reports by 300 kcal will get a TDEE estimate 300 kcal too low, and the resulting target will be 300 kcal too low in the same direction, so the *predicted weight trajectory stays correct* while the absolute numbers are both wrong. The system is self-consistent and still works for rate control; it just cannot be used to report a true TDEE. This is the single most important caveat to put in front of a user.
- The 4× rule (reduce initial TDEE estimate by 4× the weekly %-loss goal) is a directly implementable, published constant, and it exists because aggressive goals systematically break the month-1 estimate. For a user targeting 1%/week, start ~10% below the formula estimate. **This closely matches the user's existing profile note ("start calories ~10% under formula, calibrate from scale") — the two are the same correction, independently arrived at.**
- The fat/lean energy densities (4,282 and 824 kcal/lb) mean the kcal-per-kg conversion depends on body composition of the tissue lost, which is unobservable without DEXA. For a high-body-fat user (>30% BF, as here), assuming a high fat fraction (0.85-0.95) is reasonable and makes the conversion closer to ~3,800-4,100 kcal/lb than 3,500. Using 3,500 for a high-BF user will *overestimate* expected loss and trigger spurious calorie cuts.

### Gaps
- No published validation study comparing any app's expenditure algorithm against doubly-labelled water or metabolic-chamber values. MacroFactor acknowledges that "the default method of quantifying the accuracy of something like MacroFactor's expenditure algorithm is to run a validation study" and I found no published results of one for any app in this space.
- No source gives a kcal-per-kg-lost figure for the expenditure drop itself (MacroFactor explicitly declines to).
- No app documents an algorithmic under-reporting correction. If one exists commercially, it is not published.
- Thomas DM's models were in the search landscape but I did not retrieve a primary Thomas paper, so I have no Thomas-specific parameters or comparison against Hall's model to report.

## Plateau definition

### Takeaway
There is no standard literature definition of a weight-loss plateau; consumer-health sources converge loosely on 3-4 weeks of no change, the only quantified app-side threshold is MacroFactor's "3-4 weeks of persistent stall gives much more confidence in a real expenditure change," and the dynamic-modelling literature reframes the plateau as an expected asymptote rather than a failure.

### Cited Findings
- "Researchers don't use one universal definition"; a 2026 review describes a plateau broadly as a period when weight loss slows or stops despite continued efforts — [Noom](https://www.noom.com/?p=26032921) (consumer source, and I could not retrieve the underlying review)
- Common clinical rules of thumb, all from consumer-health publishers and mutually inconsistent: "weight has stayed the same for **three to four weeks** despite your efforts" — [MedicineNet](https://www.medicinenet.com/normal_to_plateau_in_weight_loss_how_long_lasts/article.htm); "at least **four weeks** since I've seen any change" — [WeightWatchers](https://www.weightwatchers.com/us/blog/how-to-know-if-youve-hit-a-weight-loss-plateau-and-what-to-do-about-it); "little or no change in body weight for **several weeks**" — [ASDA Online Doctor](https://onlinedoctor.asda.com/uk/weight-loss-plateaus.html)
- A plateau is commonly reported to last **two to six weeks** — [The National](https://thenationalnews.com/lifestyle/wellbeing/2025/01/03/weight-loss-plateau-metabolism) (consumer source attributing this to unnamed "experts")
- Timing of onset: plateaus "often begin around **six to eight months (26 to 35 weeks)**" into a weight-loss program, per a 2026 review reported second-hand — [Noom](https://www.noom.com/?p=26032921). **I could not locate the primary review; treat as unverified.**
- NCBI Bookshelf carries a chapter on the topic — [NBK576400](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK576400/) (surfaced in search; I did not retrieve its text, so I cannot quote a definition from it)
- Modelling view: the plateau is the predicted asymptote of energy equilibrium, reached at **~1.4 years** in Hall's worked example — [PMC3880593](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880593)
- App-side operational threshold: a stall or surge "persisting for **3-4 weeks**" is what gives MacroFactor "much more confidence in a real expenditure change" — [MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)
- Sigma Nutrition explicitly declines to give a numeric plateau threshold and instead reframes plateaus as "practice for weight maintenance rather than failure" — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Stronger By Science's diet guide **never defines a plateau** across its full 120,000+ characters; it only says "if weight isn't changing at the desired rate, adjust the calorie target" — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)

### Inferences
- The 3-4 week convergence (consumer rules of thumb, MacroFactor's confidence window, and the noise analysis in question 1) is not a coincidence: 3-4 weeks is simply how long it takes for a ~0.5 kg/week signal to clear a ~0.8 kg daily noise floor. **A plateau is best defined statistically, not by a fixed calendar window.** The implementable version: a plateau exists when the confidence interval on the trend slope over the last N days includes zero, *and* N is large enough that the interval is narrower than the target rate. With daily weights this is testable from the data the app already has.
- For this app, the honest operational definition is a two-part test: (1) trend slope over 14-21 days is less than ~30-50% of target rate, **and** (2) logged intake over the same window averaged within ~10% of target. Part (2) is what separates a real plateau from a logging/adherence plateau, and without it the app will cut calories for the wrong reason.
- Because "plateau" has no literature definition, the app should avoid the word in user-facing copy and instead state what it measured ("trend −0.1 kg/week over 18 days vs target −0.7 kg/week"). This is both more honest and more actionable.

### Gaps
- **I found no peer-reviewed source that operationalises a plateau with a specific threshold and window.** Every numeric figure above traces to a consumer-health publisher or an unretrieved secondary report. This is a genuine hole in the literature, not a search failure — the term appears to be clinical vernacular rather than a defined construct.
- The 2026 review behind the "6-8 months" onset figure and the semaglutide 60-week plateau figure could not be located; both should be verified before use.

## Adherence and feedback: does frequent self-weighing plus automated feedback help?

### Takeaway
The evidence is positive but conditional: trials that paired daily weighing with tailored automated feedback produced clinically significant extra weight loss (one reported ~6.1 kg more for daily vs less-frequent weighers) and no adverse psychological effects, while a trial that instructed daily weighing *without* feedback found no benefit — so the feedback loop, not the weighing, appears to be the active ingredient.

### Cited Findings
- **WEIGH trial:** 91 overweight adults randomised to a 6-month daily self-weighing program vs delayed-intervention control. Feedback came from a smart scale that uploaded weights to a website, with **weekly emails providing tailored feedback**. Outcomes at 3 and 6 months. Authors concluded the intervention "can produce clinically significant weight loss" — [Steinberg et al., PubMed 23512320](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23512320/); [trial registration NCT01369004](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01369004)
- **Dose-response within WEIGH:** "weighing everyday led to greater adoption of weight control behaviors and produced greater weight loss compared to weighing most days of the week" — [PubMed 25683820](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25683820/); [PMC4380831](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4380831/)
- Effect size as reported by a secondary source: in a 6-month randomised trial, daily weighers lost **~6.1 kg more** than less-frequent weighers — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/). (This is a secondary citation; the primary is likely the WEIGH dose-response analysis above, but I did not read the primary to confirm the exact figure.)
- **Psychological safety:** a separate analysis of the same trial reported that "the daily self-weighing intervention does not cause adverse psychological outcomes" — [PMC4157390](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4157390)
- **Visual feedback:** a trial specifically on frequent self-weighing plus visual feedback in overweight adults — [PMC4443883](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4443883/)
- **Negative trial:** a UK RCT of 183 obese adults given scales and told to weigh daily found "no evidence that self-weighing frequency was associated with more weight loss." Both arms received two weight-loss consultations, which the authors described as ineffective, so the self-weighing effect was isolated — [PMC4195875](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4195875/)
- **Incentives don't substitute for feedback:** a 2-arm trial of 258 adults in an online program found escalating lottery-based incentives "transiently increased rates of self-weighing but did not significantly enhance weight loss maintenance" vs weekly messages — [PMC6826643](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6826643)
- **Observational maintenance data:** among people who kept significant weight loss for at least one year, **44% weighed themselves daily** — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Feedback cadence actually tested in the positive trials: **daily weighing + weekly tailored feedback** — [PubMed 23512320](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23512320/)
- MacroFactor's own frequency guidance matches: "once per week is the minimum, but **daily is preferable**" for weight — [MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/109-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-weight-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates)

### Inferences
- The contrast between the WEIGH trials and the UK trial is the most decision-relevant finding in this section: **daily weighing is an input to a feedback system, not an intervention by itself.** An app that only records and charts weights should not be expected to reproduce the WEIGH effect sizes; the tailored weekly feedback message is the part that was tested.
- The tested cadence is **daily data in, weekly feedback out**. That is an unusually clean specification for an app: smooth daily, but surface a narrative/decision only once a week. It also matches MacroFactor's weekly check-in architecture and avoids the obvious harm of giving a user a verdict on noise.
- The "no adverse psychological outcomes" finding was measured in the context of *tailored, supportive* feedback. It does not license presenting raw daily fluctuations without interpretation, and it is not evidence about users with disordered-eating history — the trials recruited overweight adults in a structured program.
- The incentive trial suggests that engagement mechanics (streaks, rewards) raise logging rates without improving outcomes. Useful for prioritisation: spend effort on the quality of the weekly feedback, not on gamifying the weigh-in.

### Gaps
- I did not find a trial that varies **feedback cadence** (e.g. daily vs weekly vs biweekly automated feedback) as the experimental manipulation, so "weekly is best" is the cadence that was *tested*, not the cadence that was shown to be *optimal*.
- No trial found that tests automated feedback driven by an algorithmic TDEE estimate (as opposed to generic tailored messages).
- No evidence found on subjective-rating (hunger/energy/sleep) logging as part of an automated feedback loop. This appears to be a genuinely unstudied area — the coaching frameworks use these ratings, but I found no RCT evidence for them.

## Safety rails: floors, maximum rates, stopping criteria, red flags in subjective data

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced rail is the rate of loss — Garthe's RCT in elite athletes found 0.7%/week preserved and increased lean mass while 1.4%/week did not — and there are well-specified macronutrient floors (notably a fat floor formula), but **no source in this research gives an absolute calorie floor**, and no source gives validated numeric thresholds for subjective red flags.

### Cited Findings — rate of loss
- **Garthe et al. 2011 RCT:** slow rate (SR) = **0.7% bodyweight/week** vs fast rate (FR) = **1.4%/week** in elite athletes. "LBM increased in SR by **2.1% ± 0.4% (p < .001)**, whereas it was unchanged in FR (**−0.2% ± 0.7%**), with significant differences between groups (p < .01)." Energy intake was cut by **19% ± 2% (SR) and 30% ± 4% (FR)** — [PubMed 21896944](https://www.pubmed.gov/21896944/)
- Fat loss was **31% (SR) vs 21% (FR)** in the 2011 paper; a 2009 abstract of apparently the same work reports 31% vs 23.4% and a non-significant +1.1% LBM in the fast group. **These figures conflict across records and I only saw abstracts** — [sponet record 4017680](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4017680)
- **12-month follow-up weakens the conclusion:** "BM decreased by approximately 6% in both groups during the intervention but was not different from baseline values after 12 mo," and "there were no significant differences between groups after 12 mo, suggesting that WL rate is not the most important factor in maintaining BC and performance after WL in elite athletes" — [agris record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122535/records/65df7eb30f3e94b9e5d93233); [duo.uio.no](https://duo.uio.no/handle/10852/28711)
- **Thyroid/BMR arm (Koivisto, Sundgot-Borgen, Andersen, Garthe, n=30):** the fast group showed a significant drop in free T4 that the slow group did not; however "BMR was reduced significantly for both SLOW and FAST, but the changes did not differ between the intervention groups." Authors still recommended 0.7%/week for body composition — [sponet record 4017680](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4017680)
- Rate guidance from Stronger By Science: **0.25-1.0%/week** is slow-to-moderate; **>1.0%/week** is aggressive and the article says more conservative intakes are "generally more advisable" — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- General population ceiling: "for healthy, sustainable weight loss, it is generally not advisable to lose more than **2 pounds** of fat" per week — [NASM Body Weight Planner](https://nasm.org/nasm-body-weight-planner)
- The safe rate declines with leanness — multiple coaching sources agree, e.g. one prep-coaching guideline starts at 1-1.5%/week and drops "to around **0.7%** as the athlete nears the show." Coaching source, not research — [Peak Physique](https://skool.com/peak-physique/setting-the-right-rate-of-fat-loss)
- One frequently cited ceiling on fat mobilisation: **~31 kcal per pound of body fat per day** (attributed to a 2005 analysis by Alpert), above which muscle loss becomes more likely — [Muscle For Life](https://www.muscleforlife.com/lose-fat-fast/). **Secondary source; I did not verify the primary.**

### Cited Findings — macronutrient floors (well specified)
- **Fat floor:** `(height_cm − 150) × 0.5 + 30 g/day`, with a flat **30 g/day** for anyone under 150 cm. The older rule of ~0.5-0.6 g/kg is described as "less optimal" but still a common rough floor — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- **Protein:** lifters **1.6-2.2 g/kg bodyweight** (higher may suit very lean people in a deficit); non-lifters 1.2-1.8 g/kg. Alternative basis: 2-2.75 g/kg fat-free mass, with documented extremes of ~1.25 to 3.1 g/kg FFM — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- **Carbohydrate:** at least **3-4 g/kg/day** for lifters if high-intensity performance matters and calories allow; at least 6 g/kg/day for endurance athletes. Low-carb ≤30% of energy or ~200 g/day; ketogenic typically ≤50-60 g/day — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- **Fibre:** **14 g per 1,000 kcal**, or 28 g/day (women) to 36 g/day (men); most people find 20-50 g/day comfortable — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- **Hydration:** 3-4 L/day for most people — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- Protein's effect plateaus at ~**1.6 g/kg** when resistance training is limited to three days/week, per one review cited second-hand — [Muscle For Life](https://www.muscleforlife.com/lose-fat-fast/)
- Algorithmic implementation of floors: MacroFactor protects **fat first at a level considered safe, then protein, then carbs** when calories are very low, and fat "won't drop below a minimum level" — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/); [MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)

### Cited Findings — mechanistic red flags
- Stronger By Science names three specific consequences of over-cutting, which function as the mechanistic basis for red flags: **too little fat → impaired sex hormone production; too little carbohydrate → impaired exercise performance; too little protein → impaired lean mass retention** — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/diet/)
- Stress/cortisol confound: cortisol can increase water retention and mask fat loss; in one study, high dietary restraint was associated with **~15% higher basal cortisol** vs low restraint — [Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/scale-paradox/)
- Behavioural guard found in a coaching source: **"avoid cutting after a poor-sleep or stress week."** Self-described rule of thumb, not measured — [Coachway](https://coachway.io/articles/how-to-adjust-a-clients-calories-after-two-weeks/)
- Diet-break implementation: MacroFactor's method is to switch the goal to maintain, or keep the goal and set calories to estimated expenditure while largely ignoring macro targets — [MacroFactor algorithms & philosophy](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/)
- Reverse-diet exit pace (illustrative, not Norton): **~50 kcal every few weeks** until intake holds weight, over several weeks to several months — [Today's Dietitian](https://www.todaysdietitian.com/ask-the-expert-reverse-dieting/)

### Inferences
- For this user (134 kg, BF >30%), 0.7%/week ≈ 0.94 kg/week and 1.0%/week ≈ 1.34 kg/week. The Garthe evidence base was elite athletes who were already lean; at >30% BF the rate ceiling can defensibly sit at the upper end (0.7-1.0%/week) because there is far more fat mass available to mobilise, and the Alpert-style "kcal per lb of fat" framing points the same way. The 12-month Garthe follow-up finding that rate "is not the most important factor" further argues against treating 0.7% as a hard cap.
- Floors are the natural *hard* rails to implement, because they are computable and absolute. The fat formula gives a concrete number for any height; protein follows from bodyweight; the remainder is the carb budget. **This yields an implied calorie floor without needing a published one:** `floor_kcal = 9 × fat_floor_g + 4 × protein_g + 4 × minimum_carb_g`. For a 2 m, 134 kg user: fat floor = (200−150) × 0.5 + 30 = **55 g**; protein at 1.8 g/kg ≈ 240 g. That alone is ~1,460 kcal before any carbohydrate. This is a far better-grounded floor than any round number like 1,200 or 1,500 kcal, because it is derived from the physiological limits that were actually published.
- The subjective 1-10 ratings have no validated thresholds, so they should be used **relationally, not absolutely**: compare the current 7-day mean of each rating against the user's own 28-day baseline, and gate the *direction* of adjustment rather than issue a verdict. A defensible rule built only from what is sourced: block a calorie *reduction* when the 7-day mean of sleep or energy has dropped by more than ~1.5 points vs baseline, or when hunger has risen by more than ~1.5 points — this operationalises the sourced "avoid cutting after a poor-sleep or stress week" guard and the cortisol/water-masking finding, which together say that a stall during a high-stress week is the least reliable signal to act on. The 1.5-point magnitude is my own choice, not a sourced threshold.
- Training performance is the one subjective-adjacent input that has a sourced mechanism (low carbohydrate → impaired performance), so a drop in training performance should preferentially trigger *carbohydrate* restoration rather than a general calorie change. Similarly, a hormone/libido/mood red flag maps to the *fat* floor. Routing red flags to the specific macro with the sourced mechanism is more defensible than routing everything to total calories.
- Stopping criteria can be assembled from floors rather than invented: stop or break the diet when (a) the target intake would breach the computed macro floors, (b) the trend has stalled for 3-4 weeks at verified adherence *and* calories are already at the conservative end, or (c) subjective red flags persist for 2+ weeks. Only (a) is fully sourced; (b) and (c) are constructions.

### Gaps
- **No source gives an absolute minimum calorie floor.** Stronger By Science explicitly gives no calorie floor across the entire guide; MacroFactor says fat won't drop below "a minimum level" without naming it. The commonly quoted 1,200 kcal (women) / 1,500 kcal (men) figures did not appear in any source I retrieved and should not be cited as evidence-based on the basis of this research.
- **No validated numeric thresholds exist for 1-10 subjective ratings.** No source gives a cut-off on hunger, energy, sleep or mood that triggers an action. The coaching frameworks use these ratings but publish no thresholds, and I found no RCT. Any number the app uses here is a design choice and should be labelled as such in the code.
- The 0.7%/week figure's generalisability to a high-body-fat non-athlete is **not** established; Garthe studied elite athletes and the 12-month follow-up contradicted the short-term conclusion. Reported figures also conflict between the 2009 abstract and 2011 paper, and I read only abstracts/catalogue records.
- The Alpert ~31 kcal/lb-of-fat/day ceiling comes from a secondary fitness source; I did not verify the 2005 primary analysis or its methodology.
- Diet-break and refeed protocols with specific numbers (duration, frequency, calorie level) were **not** found. Stronger By Science's guide does not cover them; MacroFactor only describes the mechanism, not a protocol. The MATADOR trial would be the obvious primary source but did not surface in these searches.
- No source gives red-flag thresholds for stopping a diet based on performance decrement (e.g. "% drop in top set").
