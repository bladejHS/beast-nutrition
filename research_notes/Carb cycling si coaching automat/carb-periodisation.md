# Carbohydrate periodisation ("carb cycling") and training-day carbohydrate targets in a fat-loss phase

**Scope note for the report writer.** Subject profile given in the brief: male, 23 y, ~134 kg, BF >30%, calorie deficit, 5–6 sessions/week (resistance + easy aerobic), no competition. Memory/profile notes give height ~2 m, which puts BMI at ~33.5, **not >40** as stated in the brief's key question 5 — flag this discrepancy; the "BMI >40" framing changes nothing in the evidence but the arithmetic below uses 134 kg / ~2 m.
**Headline for the engineer:** every number below that is expressed in g/kg comes from research on *lean endurance or strength athletes in energy balance or surplus*. For a 134 kg man in a deficit, the g/kg bands are arithmetically unreachable and must be used as **ceilings / sanity checks**, not targets; the binding constraint is the energy budget minus protein and a fat floor. All arithmetic marked "own calculation" is mine, not from a source.

## Q1. What do position stands and meta-analyses say about daily carbohydrate by training load? (exact bands + source)

### Takeaway
The canonical four bands (3–5 / 5–7 / 6–10 / 8–12 g/kg body mass/day) are published verbatim in the 2016 Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics / Dietitians of Canada / ACSM joint position stand *Nutrition and Athletic Performance*, keyed to daily exercise duration and intensity, not to "hard/easy" labels; ISSN's nutrient-timing stand independently gives 8–12 g/kg/day only as the intake that *maximises* glycogen. No position stand gives a carbohydrate band for an obese, non-competing trainee in a deficit.

### Cited Findings
- The 2016 AND/DC/ACSM joint position stand, Table 1 ("Daily needs for fuel and recovery"), gives four training-load rows with these carbohydrate targets, verbatim from the full text: **Light** — "low intensity or skill-based activities" — **3–5 g/kg of athlete's BW/d**; **Moderate** — "moderate exercise program (e.g., ~1 h/d)" — **5–7 g/kg/d**; **High** — "endurance program (e.g., 1–3 h/d mod–high-intensity exercise)" — **6–10 g/kg/d**; **Very high** — "extreme commitment (e.g., >4–5 h/d mod–high intensity exercise)" — **8–12 g/kg/d** — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016 position stand, full text PDF](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf) (abstract: [PubMed 26917108](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26917108/)).
- The same stand's own summary sentence compresses this to: "Recommendations for carbohydrate intake typically range from **3 to 10 g/kg BW/d (and up to 12 g/kg BW/d for extreme and prolonged activities)**, depending on the fuel demands of training or competition" — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- The same table's "acute fuelling" rows: general fuelling for events <90 min — **7–12 g/kg per 24 h**; carbohydrate loading for events >90 min — **36–48 h of 10–12 g/kg BW per 24 h**; "speedy refuelling" when <8 h separates two fuel-demanding sessions — **1–1.2 g/kg/h for the first 4 h, then resume daily fuel needs** — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- The stand explicitly states the periodisation principle in three tiers: (1) hit the carbohydrate targets and time them when session quality matters; (2) "when exercise quality or intensity is less important, it may be less important to achieve these carbohydrate targets … carbohydrate intake may be chosen to suit energy goals, food preferences, or food availability"; (3) low carbohydrate availability may be *deliberately* achieved when "the focus is on enhancing the training stimulus or adaptive response" — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- ISSN position stand on nutrient timing (Kerksick et al., *JISSN* 2017;14:33): "Endogenous glycogen stores are maximized by following a high-carbohydrate diet (**8–12 g of carbohydrate/kg/day**); moreover, these stores are depleted most by high volume exercise." — [Europe PMC / PMID 28919842](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28919842); [full text](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4).
- The 2016 stand says macronutrients "should be expressed using guidelines per kg body mass to allow recommendations to be scaled to the large range in the body sizes of athletes" — i.e. per-kg scaling is an explicit convention, justified by athlete body-size range (not by adiposity) — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- Strength/power sports have no equivalent carbohydrate band. Slater & Phillips' IOC-linked guideline paper for strength sports concluded that intakes of strength-power athletes "are generally high but intakes tend to be unremarkable when expressed relative to body mass", that distribution data are "not readily available", and that "there is a general void of scientific investigation relating specifically to this unique group of athletes" — [Slater & Phillips 2011, *J Sports Sci* / PMID 21660839](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21660839).
- Resistance-training-specific review: "although severe carbohydrate restriction may not impair strength adaptations during a resistance training program, consuming an adequate amount of carbohydrate in the days leading up to testing may enhance maximal strength and strength-endurance performance", and effects of chronic low-carbohydrate resistance training on hypertrophy "are conflicting and require further research to determine a minimal carbohydrate threshold" — [Cholewa, Newmire & Zanchi 2019, *Nutrition* / PMID 30586657](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30586657).
- Real-world descriptive benchmark for a mixed resistance+aerobic, intermittent-sport population (doubly labelled water, n=6 English Premier League players): carbohydrate intake was **6.4 ± 2.2 g/kg on match days vs 4.2 ± 1.4 g/kg on training days**; energy intake 3789 kcal (61.1 kcal/kg LBM) on match days vs 2956 kcal (45.2 kcal/kg LBM) on training days; protein and fat showed **no** day-to-day periodisation — [Anderson et al. 2017, *IJSNEM* 27(3):228–238](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2016-0259).

### Inferences
- The four bands are **duration-indexed, not intensity-indexed**: "~1 h/d" maps to 5–7 g/kg regardless of whether that hour is lifting or cycling. An algorithm should therefore key the band off *session duration × intensity*, with 3–5 g/kg as the floor band for skill/low-intensity days and rest days.
- Mapping the subject onto the table: 5–6 sessions/week of ~60–90 min would place him in the "Moderate" to low "High" row, nominally 5–7 g/kg BM/day. **Own calculation:** at 134 kg that is 670–938 g carbohydrate/day = 2680–3752 kcal from carbohydrate alone — more than his likely total intake in a deficit. This is the single most important engineering finding: *the position-stand bands cannot be applied to total body mass in this subject.*
- Anderson et al. is the closest published analogue of "hard day vs easy day" carbohydrate in a mixed-modality athlete, and the ~1.5× ratio (6.4 vs 4.2 g/kg) is a defensible shape for a day-type multiplier — but it is descriptive of elite lean players in energy balance, not prescriptive, and it was spontaneous intake, not an intervention.
- For resistance-dominant training there is **no position-stand band at all**; practitioners import the endurance bands. Any resistance-day number in the app is extrapolation and should be labelled as such.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the original source of the four-band table. Secondary sources (a GSSI handout surfaced in search, the NSCA "Carbohydrate Periodization Part 1" article) attribute it to **Burke, Hawley, Wong & Jeukendrup, "Carbohydrates for training and competition", *J Sports Sci* 2011;29(S1):S17–S27**, but both those pages were inaccessible (404 / 403) and I did not read the 2011 paper. The 2016 position-stand text above is verified and should be the citation used. Burke's earlier 2001 review reportedly gave only 5–7 g/kg/d (general training) and 7–10 g/kg/d (endurance) — unverified, from search snippet only.
- No IOC consensus statement text was retrieved (the 2016 position stand is the joint ACSM document; the IOC consensus is a separate publication I could not access).
- No meta-analysis exists that compares daily carbohydrate *levels* against body-composition or strength outcomes in a deficit — the bands are consensus/mechanism-based, not meta-analytic.

## Q2. Does cycling carbohydrate between days (same weekly total) improve fat loss or performance vs even distribution?

### Takeaway
No. Every controlled comparison I found — including the only meta-analysis, the only trial in people with obesity, and the only within-day periodisation RCT — reports **no advantage of day-to-day carbohydrate cycling over an even distribution** at matched energy/carbohydrate. The single trial that reported a benefit (Campbell 2020 refeed study) had its conclusion formally challenged in a published reanalysis. Carb cycling is best treated as an adherence/compliance tool, not a metabolic one.

### Cited Findings
- **Meta-analysis (performance, the strongest evidence):** nine studies in endurance-trained athletes (VO2max ≥55 female / ≥60 male ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, interventions ≥1 week, periodised CHO restriction ≥3×/week). Result: "**no overall effect of CHO periodization on endurance performance compared to control endurance training with normal (high) CHO availability (standardized mean difference = 0.17 [−0.15, 0.49]; P = 0.29)**"; conclusion: "periodized CHO restriction does not per se enhance performance in endurance-trained athletes" — [Gejl & Nybo 2021, *JISSN* / PMID 34001184](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-021-00435-3).
- **Carb cycling in obesity (most directly relevant RCT):** 30 women with obesity (BMI 30–45), 12 weeks, both arms prescribed a 25% deficit and 1.2 g/kg/day protein; one arm continuous (CCR), one arm with structured **carbohydrate refeeds + planned diet breaks** (DRF). Body-fat % fell 6.7 ± 2.1% (CCR) vs 6.0 ± 1.9% (DRF); fat mass −9.30 ± 2.77 kg vs −9.21 ± 2.63 kg, "between-group differences were negligible (p > 0.05; Cohen's d = 0.03)"; the interaction for FFM was non-significant (p = 0.08). Notably the continuous arm actually achieved a **larger** daily deficit (−1005 ± 515 vs −690 ± 120 kcal/day, p = 0.041) — i.e. the cycling arm lost the same fat on less restriction — [Johnson, Campbell et al. 2026, *Nutrients* / PMID 42280466](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42280466).
- **The pro-cycling trial:** 27 resistance-trained adults (80 ± 15.6 kg), 7 weeks, ~25% deficit, 4 resistance sessions/week. Refeed arm = 2 consecutive days of elevated carbohydrate + 5 days restricted each week. Body mass and fat mass fell equally (RF −3.2 kg BM / −2.8 kg FM; CN −3.6 kg BM / −2.3 kg FM, no group difference), but FFM (−0.4 vs −1.3 kg, p = 0.006), dry FFM (−0.2 vs −1.9 kg) and RMR (−38 vs −78 kcal) were "better maintained" in the refeed arm — [Campbell et al. 2020, *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 5(1):19](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk5010019).
- **Published reanalysis of that trial:** a formal comment in the same journal re-analysed the data and concluded that **only dry fat-free mass differed between groups**, contradicting the paper's stated conclusions — [Peos, Brown, Vorland, Allison & Sainsbury 2020, *JFMK* 5(4):85](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk5040085); authors' reply: [Campbell 2020, *JFMK* 5(4):86](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk5040086). An independent practitioner critique additionally notes post-testing occurred immediately after a refeed, so glycogen/water shifts could inflate the refeed group's FFM, and that the refeed group ate non-significantly *fewer* carbohydrates overall — [Henselmans review](https://mennohenselmans.com/campbell-refeed-study-review/) (non-peer-reviewed; treat as commentary).
- **Diet break (carbohydrate-driven refeed) in resistance-trained athletes:** 26 resistance-trained athletes (21.3 ± 7.5% body fat) after 12 weeks of intermittent energy restriction took a 1-week diet break in which "energy intake was increased (by means of increased carbohydrate intake) to predicted weight maintenance requirements". Result: **no significant effect on fat mass**; +0.6 kg body weight, +0.7 kg FFM, REE 7000 → 7200 kJ/day (p = 0.026); leg muscle endurance improved (quadriceps 25-rep work 2530 → 2660 J, p = 0.018) but **strength did not change**; hunger and irritability fell, fullness/satisfaction/alertness rose — [Peos et al. 2021, *PLoS ONE* / PMID 33630880](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33630880).
- **Within-day carbohydrate periodisation RCT:** 42 trained men, 4 weeks, resistance + HIIT, three arms (Sleep-Low/No-CHO with all carbohydrate before evening training; Sleep-High low-GI; Sleep-High high-GI). Body fat fell 1.5% and 1.4 kg, FFM rose 0.9 kg, VO2max/CMJ/1RM all improved — "**There were no significant differences among interventions**"; conclusion: "a balanced dietary plan with sufficient nutrient and energy intake promotes body composition optimization and physical fitness, **independently of carbohydrate type or timing**" — [Vlahoyiannis et al. 2024, *Eur J Nutr* / PMID 39585451](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/39585451).
- **Position-stand level verdict on intermittent patterns:** "The collective body of intermittent caloric restriction research demonstrates **no significant advantage over daily caloric restriction** for improving body composition." Same stand: "A wide range of dietary approaches (low-fat to low-carbohydrate/ketogenic, and all points between) can be similarly effective for improving body composition", and "the higher the baseline body fat level, the more aggressively the caloric deficit may be imposed" — [Aragon et al. 2017, ISSN position stand: diets and body composition, *JISSN* / PMID 28630601](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28630601).
- Consumer/clinical summaries agree the evidence base is thin and athlete-focused: Harvard Health notes most carb-cycling research is in competitive athletes and that any rapid scale change is partly glycogen-bound water — [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/diet-and-nutrition/carb-cycling-what-is-it-and-does-it-work) (low evidence tier; use only as a "what the public is told" reference).

### Inferences
- With energy and weekly carbohydrate matched, the expected effect of day-to-day carb cycling on fat loss is **zero**. The honest marketing claim is: cycling may improve *adherence* (Peos 2021's appetite/alertness findings) and lets you put the available carbohydrate where it buys training quality — nothing more.
- The one "cycling preserves lean mass" result in the literature survives only as *dry* fat-free mass after reanalysis, and is confounded by measuring immediately post-refeed. An algorithm must not promise lean-mass preservation from refeeds.
- Johnson 2026 is an important practical caveat in the other direction: the cycling arm achieved its fat loss with a **smaller** average deficit. That is compatible with cycling improving adherence/energy expenditure enough to offset less restriction — worth stating as a hypothesis, not a finding.
- Anderson 2017 (Q1) shows elite practitioners *do* cycle carbohydrate day-to-day while holding protein and fat constant. That is the correct structural pattern for an algorithm: **vary carbohydrate, hold protein fixed, hold fat at/above a floor**.

### Gaps
- I found **no** trial of day-to-day carbohydrate cycling (hard/easy/rest-day tiers at matched weekly total) in overweight or obese resistance trainees. Campbell 2020 and Johnson 2026 test weekly *refeeds/diet breaks*, which is a coarser manipulation.
- No trial has tested whether carbohydrate cycling improves *resistance-training performance* (volume load, RPE) across a week in a deficit — the mechanism most often claimed for it.
- No study measured adherence/dropout as a primary outcome for carb cycling, so the adherence rationale is itself unevidenced.

## Q3. "Train low" / sleep-low / periodised carbohydrate availability: effects on performance, adaptation, fat oxidation — and the risks

### Takeaway
Train-low reliably amplifies the *molecular* signals of endurance adaptation (cell signalling, gene expression, fat-oxidation enzymes) but translates into measured performance gains in only a minority of studies and in **none** of the meta-analysed ones; it consistently and predictably **reduces training intensity** when glycogen falls too low, and repetitive low-carbohydrate training has been linked to increased illness susceptibility. It is an endurance-athlete adaptation tool, with no demonstrated relevance to resistance training or fat loss in people with obesity.

### Cited Findings
- **Framework and definition.** Impey et al.'s "fuel for the work required" model defines CHO availability as endogenous (muscle + liver glycogen) plus exogenous carbohydrate "available to sustain the required training intensity and duration", and proposes that "CHO availability should be manipulated **day-to-day and meal-by-meal** according to the intensity, duration and specific training goals", as opposed to chronic restriction. Key sessions and competition "should always be undertaken with high CHO availability so as to promote performance and recovery". The practical model is a per-meal red/amber/green (HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW) schedule across pre-training meal, during training, post-training meal and evening meal — [Impey et al. 2018, *Sports Med*, doi:10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7) (open access; accepted manuscript at [LJMU repository](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/8142/)).
- **Glycogen threshold numbers (the only quantitative dose-response in the field).** From Impey et al.: adaptations are "particularly evident when absolute pre-exercise muscle glycogen concentrations are ≤ **300 mmol/kg dw**"; restoring post-exercise glycogen to **>500 mmol/kg dw** attenuates exercise-induced gene expression; keeping glycogen/energy at "critically low post-exercise levels (i.e. **<100 mmol/kg dw**) may reduce the regulation of protein synthesis pathways"; and "commencing exercise with **<200 mmol/kg dw** is likely to impair training intensity due to the lack of muscle substrate and impact on the contractile capacity of muscle cells via impaired calcium regulation" — [Impey et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7).
- **Outcome tally (Impey et al., Table 2, train-low studies).** Signalling: 73% positive (8/11), 27% no change, 0% negative. Exercise performance changes: **37% positive (4/11), 63% no/equivalent change (7/11), 0% negative**. Efficiency/economy: 1 of 2 positive. **Impaired training intensity: 100% negative (3/3)** (Yeo et al., Hulston et al., Lane et al.) — [Impey et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7).
- **Illness risk.** "Repetitive daily training in the face of a reduction in CHO availability (so as to reduce pre-exercise muscle glycogen concentration) **may increase susceptibility to illness**" — [Impey et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7) (citing their ref 53; this is a single-citation statement in a Current Opinion paper, i.e. weak evidence).
- **Meta-analysed performance verdict:** no overall effect (SMD 0.17, 95% CI −0.15 to 0.49, p = 0.29, 9 studies); the review names "compromised training quality and particularly **lower intensities in peak intervals**" as "a major drawback from dietary interventions with chronic carbohydrate (CHO) restriction" — [Gejl & Nybo 2021](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-021-00435-3).
- **The flagship sleep-low RCT (the strongest positive result, and the one that matters for a fat-loss app):** 21 triathletes (VO2max 58.7 ± 5.7), 3 weeks; **both groups ate the same 6 g/kg/day carbohydrate**, differing only in timing (evening HIT with high CHO availability → overnight CHO restriction → next-morning low-availability session). Results: delta efficiency +11 ± 15% vs +1.4 ± 9.3% (p < 0.05); supramaximal cycling capacity at 150% peak power +12.5 ± 19.0% vs +1.63 ± 12.4% (p = 0.06); 10-km run time −2.9 ± 2.15% vs −0.10 ± 2.03% (p < 0.05); **fat mass −8.5 ± 7.4% vs −2.6 ± 7.4% (p < 0.01) with no change in lean mass** — [Marquet et al. 2016, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* / PMID 26741119](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26741119/).
- A 1-week sleep-low protocol in 11 trained cyclists reportedly improved a 20-km time trial by ~3.2% vs control, with the gain attributed to pacing rather than altered substrate use — [Marquet et al. 2016, *Nutrients* 8(12):755](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/8/12/755) (**flag: from search-result snippet/DOAJ record only — the full text 403'd for me, numbers unverified**).
- **Sherman's 1981 observation, quoted in Impey et al., is the deflationary classic:** carbohydrate loading gave "no benefit to performance during the 20.9 km run. In fact, performance times were actually slower in the trials with higher levels of muscle glycogen… anything above a minimal level of muscle glycogen is unnecessary for performance of a given intensity and duration" — [Impey et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7).
- **Position-stand caution on deliberate low availability.** The 2016 stand lists low carbohydrate availability as one of the factors whose interaction with chronic energy restriction, poor dietary quality, excessive training and dehydration has barely been studied; it warns against "practices that create unacceptably low energy availability", and notes high-fat/low-CHO feeding "may impair muscle protein synthesis" (also flagged in Impey et al.) — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf); [Impey et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7).
- Hormonal/bone/immune risk evidence I could surface is about **low energy availability (LEA/RED-S) generally, not train-low specifically**: LEA is associated with lower bone density (meta-analysis of 11 studies, 1,006 athletes, per search summary), lower testosterone in men, and persistently elevated cortisol; immune findings are inconsistent, with one 9-day intensified-training study finding immune function "robust" and a systematic review finding "definitive immune biomarkers are limited" — see [Frontiers in Endocrinology 2020 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2020.00011/pdf) and [PMC8664375](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8664375) (**flag: these were returned as search summaries; I did not read the full texts, and none of them studied train-low per se**).

### Inferences
- **Mechanism ≠ outcome.** The adaptation signal is real and replicated; the performance payoff is not. For a non-competing trainee there is no outcome that train-low is known to improve. The only train-low result that superficially matches his goal is Marquet 2016's −8.5% fat mass — from 21 lean triathletes at 6 g/kg/day in energy balance, over 3 weeks, with no mechanism established for the fat-mass difference. **Do not build a fat-loss claim on it.**
- Sleep-low's structure is nevertheless the one defensible shape for intra-day periodisation: carbohydrate concentrated around the session that needs quality, restricted in the window that follows it. In a deficit, the subject's carbohydrate is already low enough that *explicit* restriction adds risk (session quality, protein-synthesis signalling) without adding a known benefit.
- The <200 mmol/kg dw "intensity impairment" threshold is the practical guardrail: an algorithm should treat any day containing a quality resistance session as a **high-availability day**, non-negotiably, because 3/3 studies that reduced pre-session availability reduced training intensity.
- Because the subject is already in a deficit (hence plausibly low-ish energy availability), stacking deliberate train-low sessions on top is the exact combination the 2016 position stand flags as unstudied. Risk language in the app should be conservative.

### Gaps
- No train-low study in resistance training, in obesity, or in a deficit. Everything above is lean endurance athletes in roughly energy balance.
- No dose-response for "what % of sessions should be low". A review surfaced in search describes train-low being applied in "roughly 30–50% of training sessions" — I could not verify that figure in a full text, so treat it as unsourced practitioner convention.
- No direct evidence on immune/hormonal consequences of train-low itself (as distinct from LEA). The illness claim rests on a single citation inside a Current Opinion paper.
- Muscle glycogen cannot be measured non-invasively; Impey et al. themselves state practical application "relies heavily on practitioners' theoretical knowledge" and that "considerable debate is also likely to exist… as to what amount of dietary CHO actually equates to the red, amber and green (RAG) ratings". **No source converts the mmol/kg dw thresholds into grams of dietary carbohydrate.** Any such conversion in the app is invention.

## Q4. How much carbohydrate around a session — 60–90 min resistance/mixed vs long easy aerobic?

### Takeaway
For a 60–90 min resistance or mixed session the position stands say essentially **nothing is needed during** the session (<45 min: not needed; 45–75 min: mouth-rinse/small amounts), and post-session carbohydrate only matters as aggressive g/kg/h refuelling if a second fuel-demanding session falls within ~8 h. For a long easy aerobic session, intra-session carbohydrate is justified only beyond ~60–90 min at >70% VO2max (30–60 g/h), which an "easy" session by definition is not. A single resistance session depletes far less glycogen than endurance work, with a meta-analysed mean of ~104 mmol/kg dw.

### Cited Findings
- **Pre-exercise (2016 stand, Table 1):** "Before exercise >60 min — **1–4 g/kg consumed 1–4 h before exercise**." Narrative text: "Carbohydrate intakes of 1 to 4 g/kg, with timing, amount, and food choices suited to the individual, have been shown to enhance endurance or performance of prolonged exercise", with low-fat/low-fibre/low-moderate-protein choices preferred; at least **1 g/kg** in the pre-event meal is suggested specifically to offset insulin-driven suppression of fat mobilisation in people prone to premature fatigue — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- **During exercise (2016 stand, Table 1), by duration:** **<45 min — not needed**; **45–75 min — "small amounts including mouth rinse"**; **1–2.5 h — 30–60 g/h**; **>2.5–3 h — up to 90 g/h** (multiple transportable carbohydrates; the stand notes the Evidence Analysis Library found evidence for glucose+fructose benefits "currently equivocal") — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- **Post-exercise (2016 stand):** "the rate of glycogen resynthesis is only **~5% per hour**", so "early intake of carbohydrate in the recovery period (**~1 to 1.2 g/kg/h during the first 4 to 6 hours**) is useful in maximizing the effective refueling time" — but framed as applying "particularly between bouts of carbohydrate-dependent exercise where there is a priority on performance in the second session"; otherwise, "as long as total intake of carbohydrate and energy is adequate and overall nutritional goals are met, meals and snacks can be chosen from a variety of foods and fluids according to personal preferences of type and timing" — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- **Carbohydrate + protein substitution (useful in a deficit):** recovery of performance and glycogen repletion rates "were similar in athletes consuming **0.8 g carbohydrate/kg BW + 0.4 g protein/kg BW** compared with athletes consuming only carbohydrate (**1.2 g/kg BW**)" — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf). ISSN's version: if recovery <4 h, "aggressive carbohydrate refeeding (**1.2 g/kg/h**)… with a preference towards carbohydrate sources that have a high (>70) glycemic index", optionally caffeine 3–8 mg/kg, or **0.8 g/kg/h carbohydrate with 0.2–0.4 g/kg/h protein** — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28919842).
- **ISSN on prolonged work:** "Extended (>60 min) bouts of high intensity (>70% VO2max) exercise challenge fuel supply and fluid regulation, thus carbohydrate should be consumed at a rate of **~30–60 g of carbohydrate/h in a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution (6–12 fluid ounces) every 10–15 min**… particularly in those exercise bouts that span beyond 70 min" — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28919842).
- **ISSN on carbohydrate during resistance exercise:** "Carbohydrate ingestion throughout resistance exercise (e.g., 3–6 sets of 8–12 RM using multiple exercises targeting all major muscle groups) has been shown to promote euglycemia and higher glycogen stores. Consuming carbohydrate solely or in combination with protein during resistance exercise increases muscle glycogen stores, ameliorates muscle damage, and facilitates greater acute and chronic training adaptations." Note: **no gram amount is given** — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28919842).
- **How much glycogen a lifting session actually costs (meta-analysis, 2025):** 20 studies, 168 male / 12 female participants, 28 effect sizes: "a significant glycogen decrease (**MD = −104.3 mmol/kg dw; 95% CI −137.6 to −71.0; prediction interval −244.4 to 35.7; p < 0.001**)" in vastus lateralis. Meta-regression: greater depletion with more sets (−11.2 mmol per set) and longer session duration (−1.3 mmol per minute), **less** depletion at higher intensity (+2.88); varied-intensity sessions depleted more (−162.9) than fixed (−82.5); untrained more (−113.0) than trained (−101.3) — [Hamidvand et al. 2025, *Physiological Reports* / PMID 41420384](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41420384).
- **Heavy, high-volume lifting, direct measurement:** 10 elite weightlifters doing 4×5 squats at 75% 1RM + 4×5 deadlifts at 75% + 4×12 split squats depleted biochemically determined muscle glycogen by **38% (31–45%)**, with near-depletion of intramyofibrillar glycogen in a fraction of type 2 fibres — [Hokken et al. 2021, *Acta Physiol* / PMID 32961628](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32961628).
- **Real-world intra-session intake in a mixed-modality population:** EPL players took **3.1 ± 4.4 g/h during training sessions** vs **32.3 ± 21.9 g/h during matches** — i.e. practitioners feed almost nothing during ordinary training and 30+ g/h only in competition — [Anderson et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2016-0259).

### Inferences
- **Own calculation (clearly an estimate, not from a source):** converting the meta-analysed −104 mmol/kg dw to dietary grams — 104 mmol × 0.162 g/mmol glucosyl unit ≈ 17 g glycogen per kg dry muscle ≈ **~3.9 g per kg wet muscle** (dry:wet ≈ 1:4.3). A full-body session engaging on the order of 8–12 kg of muscle therefore costs roughly **30–50 g of glycogen**, i.e. 2–4% of a 134 kg man's daily intake, not hundreds of grams. **No source makes this conversion**; it is order-of-magnitude reasoning to stop the app from over-fuelling lifting days. Caveat: biopsies are vastus lateralis only, and glycogen cost ≠ carbohydrate requirement (liver glycogen, hepatic glucose output and post-session resynthesis all matter).
- The practical consequence for a 60–90 min resistance or mixed session in a deficit: **0 g during** (water/mouth-rinse at most), a normal mixed pre-session meal, and **no special post-session carbohydrate bolus** beyond hitting the day's total — because the 1–1.2 g/kg/h rule is explicitly conditioned on a second fuel-demanding session within ~8 h, which a 5–6 day/week lifting + easy cardio schedule rarely creates.
- Applying the pre-exercise band literally is another body-mass trap: **own calculation**, 1–4 g/kg at 134 kg = **134–536 g** in one pre-workout meal. These are competition-fuelling numbers for lean endurance athletes. For this subject, express pre-session carbohydrate in **absolute grams** (order 30–60 g, 1–2 h before, inside the day's budget) and label it as extrapolated.
- For a long *easy* aerobic session (low intensity, well under 70% VO2max — the subject's prescription, with jumping/running contraindicated by knees), both stands imply **no carbohydrate is needed during the session**; adding it only adds calories against the fat-loss goal. The 30–60 g/h rule should fire only if duration >75–90 min **and** intensity is genuinely moderate-high.

### Gaps
- **No source gives grams of carbohydrate needed per resistance session.** ISSN endorses carbohydrate during resistance exercise but specifies no dose; the 2016 stand's "during exercise" table is duration-based and endurance-derived.
- No study of peri-workout carbohydrate in obese trainees, and none with fat loss (rather than performance or glycogen) as the outcome.
- The glycogen-cost→dietary-carbohydrate conversion does not exist in the literature; my arithmetic above should be flagged as such in any user-facing text.

## Q5. For obesity (high BF%) doing resistance training in a deficit: total body mass, lean mass, or adjusted body weight?

### Takeaway
There is **no evidence base and no convention** for scaling carbohydrate recommendations in obesity: the position stands prescribe per-kg *total body mass* and justify it only by athlete body-size range, with no adiposity correction, and the g/kg bands become arithmetically impossible at 134 kg in a deficit. The defensible engineering answer is to stop treating g/kg as a target: compute carbohydrate as the **energy remainder** after protein (best expressed per kg fat-free mass, which *is* the convention for protein) and a fat floor, then use g/kg FFM only as a reporting/sanity metric.

### Cited Findings
- The 2016 stand's only statement on scaling: energy, carbohydrate and protein "should be expressed using guidelines **per kg body mass** to allow recommendations to be scaled to the large range in the body sizes of athletes" — body *size*, with no mention of body composition, obesity or fat mass — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- **Protein, by contrast, does have an FFM convention:** "Higher protein intakes (**2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM**) may be required to maximize muscle retention in lean, resistance-trained subjects under hypocaloric conditions" — explicitly per kg **fat-free mass** — [Aragon et al. 2017, ISSN diets and body composition](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28630601). The 2016 stand gives protein as 1.2–2.0 g/kg BW/d, with "as high as 2.0 g/kg/day or higher" in energy restriction, and cites a trial where 2.3 vs 1.0 g/kg/d over 2 weeks of energy restriction retained muscle mass while losing fat — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf).
- Rate-of-loss guidance from the same stand: aim for "a slight energy deficit to achieve a slow rather than rapid rate of loss", increase protein, and note "FFM and performance may be better preserved in athletes who **minimize weekly weight loss to <1% per week**"; the practical approach for most athletes is "decreasing energy intake by **~250 to 500 kcal/d**" — [AND/DC/ACSM 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf). ISSN adds: "The higher the baseline body fat level, the more aggressively the caloric deficit may be imposed" — [Aragon et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28630601).
- **Clinical-nutrition practice on adjusted body weight:** a review of protein requirements in obesity states that during weight loss rebuilding "is likely to need at least 1.2 g/kg body weight/d with a maximum weight of BMI 30", and that "while the protein requirement per kg body weight might be improved by expression per kg fat free mass, **adjusting body weight might be more practical**" — [Protein requirement in obesity (Amsterdam UMC repository PDF)](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/files/142148308/Protein-requirement-in-obesity.pdf).
- Dietetic-practice guidance on the formula: adjusted body weight and ideal body weight "have been used when assessing energy and protein needs in critically ill patients who have an elevated body mass index", but evidence is limited and research "has been shown to underestimate or overestimate RMR depending on the patient's weight status" — [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics practice trends](https://www.eatrightpro.org/news-center/practice-trends/adjusted-or-ideal-body-weight-for-nutrition-assessment). The standard ABW formula is IBW + 0.4 × (actual − IBW); its primary validated use is aminoglycoside dosing, not nutrition — [NPS Australian Prescriber](https://www.nps.org.au/australian-prescriber/articles/drug-dosing-in-obese-adults).
- A 12-week trial in patients with morbid obesity targeted **1.5 g protein per kg "normalized body weight"** vs 1 g/kg/d in controls and found "no significant effect on body composition" — [*Nutrients* 2024 (DOAJ record)](https://doaj.org/article/480c77873ae14940a5e23c7d02dfd492) (**flag: search-snippet only, full text not read**).
- Carbohydrate cycling specifically in obesity has one RCT and it was null (see Q2): 30 women, BMI 30–45, protein prescribed at **1.2 g/kg/day** (total body mass), refeeds/diet breaks vs continuous, fat loss identical — [Johnson, Campbell et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42280466).

### Inferences — the computable form for a 134 kg subject
**All arithmetic in this block is my own, from the cited inputs; none of it is a sourced recommendation.** Assume 134 kg, BF 32% → FM 42.9 kg, **FFM ≈ 91 kg**; height ~2.00 m → Devine IBW ≈ 93 kg → **ABW(0.4) ≈ 109 kg**.

1. **Why g/kg must not be the driver.** Lowest position-stand band (3–5 g/kg BM, "light") = **402–670 g/day** = 1,608–2,680 kcal of carbohydrate alone. Even on ABW: 328–547 g. Even on FFM: 273–455 g. A plausible deficit intake for this subject is ~2,200–2,600 kcal (profile notes say start ~10% under formula). Therefore the published bands are **unreachable at any denominator** and cannot be targets.
2. **Carbohydrate as remainder (recommended algorithm).**
   `carb_g = (kcal_budget − 4 × protein_g − 9 × fat_g) / 4`
   with `protein_g = 1.8–2.2 g/kg FFM` (≈164–200 g at 91 kg FFM; justified by the FFM convention in Aragon 2017 and the ≥2.0 g/kg-in-deficit language in the 2016 stand) and `fat_g` at a floor of ~0.5–0.7 g/kg BM (≈67–94 g) or ≥20–25% of kcal.
   Worked example at 2,400 kcal: protein 182 g (728 kcal) + fat 80 g (720 kcal) → **carb ≈ 238 g = 1.8 g/kg BM = 2.2 g/kg ABW = 2.6 g/kg FFM**.
3. **Reporting denominator.** Report and threshold on **g/kg FFM**, because (a) protein already has an FFM convention, (b) glycogen storage capacity scales with muscle mass, not fat mass, and (c) g/kg BM numbers at 134 kg are meaningless against the literature. Keep ABW only as a legacy/clinical cross-check; it has no nutrition validation.
4. **Day-type distribution (shape, not dose).** Hold protein and fat fixed (as EPL practitioners do — Anderson 2017) and vary only carbohydrate, redistributing the weekly carbohydrate pool, e.g. hard/quality resistance day ×1.25, moderate day ×1.0, easy aerobic day ×0.85, rest day ×0.75 — a ratio of roughly 1.5–1.7 between highest and lowest day, mirroring the observed 6.4 vs 4.2 g/kg match/training ratio. On a ~238 g/day average this gives ~300 / 238 / 200 / 180 g. **Expected effect on fat loss: none (Q2). Justification: adherence and training quality only.**
5. **Hard floors.** Never schedule a quality resistance session on a low day (3/3 studies in Impey's tally show reduced training intensity with low availability); keep an absolute floor of roughly **100–130 g/day** (≈1.1–1.4 g/kg FFM) so glycogen does not drift toward the <200 mmol/kg dw intensity-impairment zone, and keep the pre-session meal non-zero. These floors are extrapolated, not sourced.
6. **Rate check.** Weekly weight loss target <1% of body mass (<1.34 kg/week) per the 2016 stand; the ISSN permission to be "more aggressive" at high baseline body fat is the one place the literature explicitly favours this subject over a lean athlete.

### Gaps
- **Nothing was found** that recommends a carbohydrate g/kg figure for obese or overweight individuals, on any denominator. Europe PMC title searches for `"adjusted body weight" AND "nutrition"`, `"carbohydrate"/"energy" + "requirements" + "obesity" + "exercise"`, and `"sports nutrition" + "obesity"` returned **zero** hits. This is a genuine void, not a search failure I can fix.
- No study has validated FFM-scaled vs BM-scaled vs ABW-scaled carbohydrate prescriptions against any outcome.
- The subject's BMI (~33.5 at 2 m / 134 kg) does not match the brief's "BMI > 40" premise; if the real figure is >40 (i.e. height is lower than noted), the FFM estimate and every derived number change — the algorithm should compute FFM from a measured/estimated BF%, not from BMI.
- Fat floor (0.5–0.7 g/kg BM) is conventional practice; I found no position-stand number for it in the material retrieved.

## Evidence-level summary (for the report writer)

| Claim | Strength |
|---|---|
| 3–5 / 5–7 / 6–10 / 8–12 g/kg BM bands exist and are keyed to daily exercise duration | **High** — verbatim in AND/DC/ACSM 2016 position stand; lean athletes only |
| Day-to-day carb cycling at matched calories beats even distribution for fat loss | **Refuted / null** — 1 RCT in obesity (null, d=0.03), 1 meta-analysis for performance (SMD 0.17, p=0.29), 1 within-day RCT (null), ISSN stand (no advantage of intermittent restriction) |
| Carbohydrate refeeds preserve lean mass in a deficit | **Weak/contested** — one small trial, conclusion overturned in a published reanalysis (only dry FFM differed) |
| Train-low amplifies adaptation signalling | **High (molecular), Low (performance)** — 73% of signalling studies positive; 37% of performance studies positive; meta-analysis null |
| Train-low reduces training intensity when glycogen is too low | **Consistent** — 3/3 studies negative; mechanism threshold <200 mmol/kg dw |
| Train-low causes immune/hormonal harm | **Very weak** — single citation in a Current Opinion paper; LEA-based evidence is about energy, not carbohydrate |
| <45 min exercise needs no carbohydrate during; 30–60 g/h only >60–90 min at >70% VO2max | **High** — both position stands agree |
| 1–1.2 g/kg/h post-exercise refuelling | **High, but conditional** — only when a second fuel-demanding session falls within ~4–8 h |
| A resistance session costs ~104 mmol/kg dw glycogen (~tens of grams) | **Moderate** — 2025 meta-analysis, 20 studies, wide prediction interval, vastus lateralis only; gram conversion is mine |
| How to scale g/kg in obesity | **No evidence** — void in the literature; the remainder-based algorithm above is reasoned, not sourced |
