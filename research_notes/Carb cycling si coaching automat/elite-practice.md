# Elite practice: what athletes, coaches and practitioners actually do with carbohydrate periodisation

Scope note for the report writer: this file is the **practice-side** companion. Where a number comes from a *reported* intake (what athletes ate) rather than a *prescription* (what was recommended), it is labelled. Where I could only reach a search snippet and not the full text, that is said explicitly. Evidence tiers used throughout: **(a) supported by trials**, **(b) common practice, weak evidence**, **(c) folklore**.

Standing translation assumptions used for the 134 kg male (23 y, ~2 m, BF >30%, lifting 5-6 d/wk, in a deficit, slow metaboliser per profile):
- Lean body mass ≈ 90-94 kg (at 30-33% BF).
- g/kg **total** body mass numbers from 70-90 kg competitors do **not** transfer, because fat mass stores no glycogen; everything below is re-expressed per kg **lean mass** or in absolute grams, and then capped by the calorie budget. This is my inference, flagged as such in each section.

## Q1. Documented case studies of physique and strength athletes: weekly carbohydrate distribution, refeed/diet-break structure, outcomes

### Takeaway
The published physique case-study literature is dominated by **single male natural bodybuilders on low, fairly flat carbohydrate intakes (~1.2-2 g/kg total body mass, 20-25% of energy)** with carbohydrate used as the main adjustment lever, not as a cycled variable; structured weekly refeeds appear in *survey/interview* work and in one small RCT, but almost never in the published single-athlete case reports. Reported outcomes are consistently: large fat loss, non-trivial lean mass loss (~40% of total loss in the best-documented case), and RMR decline.

### Cited Findings

**Robinson et al. 2015, JISSN 12:20 — 14-week natural bodybuilding prep, full text read**
- Subject: 21 y male, 86.0 kg, 14% BF at baseline; placed 7th of 19 — [JISSN/PMC4422265](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Body mass 86.0 → 74.3 kg (−11.7 kg, −13.6%, ≈0.98%/week) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Fat mass −6.7 kg; **fat-free mass −5.0 kg (≈43% of the mass lost was FFM)** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Mean energy deficit 882 ± 433 kcal/d over 14 weeks — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- 14-week mean macros: **carbohydrate 100 ± 56 g/d (20 ± 3% of energy)**, fat 79 ± 17 g/d (37 ± 4%), protein 212 ± 13 g/d (45 ± 8%) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Carbohydrate was explicitly the lever adjusted to hold the energy target; the paper gives no week-by-week gram values in text (Figure 1 only) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Example weeks 1-5 menus: **training day 2,413 kcal / 137 g CHO / 119 g fat / 207 g protein; rest day 2,246 kcal / 143 g CHO / 96 g fat / 212 g protein** — i.e. carbohydrate was effectively *flat* between training and rest days; fat carried the difference — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Pre-study habitual intake: 2,128 kcal / 212 g CHO / 28 g fat / 257 g protein — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- 4 meals + 1 snack/day; late-evening low-carb high-protein snack — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- **No refeed, high-carb day, carb load or diet break was used.** The only "cheat meal" described is pre-study habitual (≈1 pizza + 200 g ice cream every two weeks) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Training: 4 resistance sessions/wk (~30 min, 6-8 exercises, 8-10 reps, 4-5 sets, 2 sessions/muscle group); HIIT + LISS cardio done **fasted at 07:00-08:00**, volume escalated as the deficit deepened (late weeks: 10 × 10-15 s sprints; 40-min incline treadmill walks); posing 2-4×/wk in final 6 weeks — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Outcomes: RMR 1,993 → 1,814 kcal/d; resting fat oxidation 0.04 → 0.06 g/min; MFO during exercise 0.24 → 0.59 g/min; Fatmax 21% → 60% VO2max; relative VO2max 49 → 46 ml/kg/min; resting HR 54 → 37 bpm — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Strength: absolute quadriceps concentric peak torque fell 293 → 273 Nm, but **relative** (Nm/kg) values mostly rose (quad concentric 3.4 → 3.7, quad eccentric 4.9 → 5.7, hamstring eccentric 2.0 → 2.9); hamstring concentric fell 1.7 → 1.5 — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- Mood (BRUMS) stayed below-average on all subscales; no hormones measured — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)

**Campbell et al. 2020, J Funct Morphol Kinesiol 5:19 — the one RCT with an explicit weekly refeed structure, full text read**
- 27 resistance-trained adults (refeed n=13, 6M/7F, 76.4 ± 15.6 kg; continuous n=14, 8M/6F, 83.1 ± 15.4 kg), ~5 y training experience, 7 weeks — [PMC7739314](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- **Structure: refeed group cut intake 35% for 5 days (typically Mon-Fri), then returned to maintenance on 2 consecutive days (typically Sat-Sun), with the added calories coming *entirely from carbohydrate*. Continuous group ran a flat 25% daily deficit.** Protein fixed at 1.8 g/kg/d in both; remaining calories split evenly between fat and CHO — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- Achieved weekly averages: refeed 1,488 kcal/d (~20 kcal/kg), **123 g CHO/d = 1.7 g/kg/d**, 130 g protein, 53 g fat, actual restriction 26%; continuous 1,617 kcal/d, 132 g CHO/d = 1.6 g/kg/d, 140 g protein, 59 g fat, actual restriction 21% — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- The paper does **not** publish the separate deficit-day vs refeed-day carbohydrate grams (a real limitation for anyone trying to copy it) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- Training: 4 d/wk supervised (2 upper, 2 lower), wk 4 a taper (2 sessions), sets 3 → 4, mostly 6-8 and 8-10 reps, ~1 rep short of failure, 25 g whey post-session, 2 × 30 min low-moderate aerobic/wk; adherence >90% — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- Outcomes (pre → post): refeed BW 76.4 → 73.2 kg, FM 16.3 → 13.5 kg, FFM 60.1 → 59.7 kg (−0.4, n.s.), dry FFM 18.7 → 18.5 kg, RMR 1,703 → 1,665 kcal (n.s.); continuous BW 83.1 → 79.5 kg, FM 16.7 → 14.4 kg, **FFM 66.4 → 65.1 kg (−1.3, p<0.05)**, dry FFM 21.9 → 20.0 kg (group×time p≤0.001), RMR 1,867 → 1,789 kcal (p<0.05) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- Fat loss did not differ significantly between groups; strength was not measured — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- **Important caveat on the headline claim:** the article page carries a published letter, "Contrary to the Conclusions Stated in the Paper, Only Dry Fat-Free Mass Was Different between Groups upon Reanalysis" — so the FFM-preservation conclusion is contested in the literature itself — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)

**Mitchell et al. 2017, Sports 5(4):76 — qualitative interviews, the best record of what competitors actually do week-to-week, full text read**
- 7 male natural bodybuilders, mean 10.4 ± 3.4 y experience; no females — [PMC5969027](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)
- Off-season example: ~4,500-5,000 kcal/d, ~**680 g CHO/d** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)
- In-season example trajectory: start ~2,400-2,500 kcal/d and ~250-275 g CHO/d → finish ~1,900-2,000 kcal/d and **~100 g CHO on low days** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)
- Protein: one athlete 250 g/d off-season (stated 2.2-2.9 g/kg); another ~225 g/d in-season (~2.3-2.4 g/kg). Fat: 100-110 g/d off-season (0.5-1.2 g/kg); in-season from ~65 g/d down by up to 25 g, ending at ~15-20% of energy — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)
- **Weekly refeed days were common in-season; one athlete describes 1 refeed day/week at approximately off-season calories (~2,800 kcal), the surplus mostly carbohydrate** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)
- Peak week: **6 of 7 used a "modified" carb load (training taper + raised carbs); 4 of 7 had previously used the classic 3-day depletion + 3-day load and moved away from it.** One athlete tapers CHO ~400 → 250 g/d then returns to ~300-400 g/d around the show — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)
- The paper reports no group means, no g/kg, no rate of weight loss — it is interview data, n=7 — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)

**Chappell, Simper & Barker 2018, JISSN 15:4 — cohort of British natural (BNBF) finalists. NOTE: full text was paywalled/403 for me; the figures below come from search-result snippets of the paper and of papers citing it, and should be verified against the PDF before publication.**
- Diet pattern was high-protein, high-carbohydrate, low-fat; **total CHO, protein and fat all fell over the prep** in both sexes — [JISSN PDF (snippet)](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12970-018-0209-z)
- **Males who placed top-5 started prep on higher carbohydrate than non-placers: 5.1 vs 3.7 g/kg BW.** Male group mean fell 4.4 → 4.1 g/kg; female 3.9 → 3.3 g/kg — [same (snippet)](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12970-018-0209-z)
- The questionnaire explicitly distinguished **"cheat meals"** (veering from the prescribed diet) from **"refeeds"** (a known amount of energy added on top of the diet) — i.e. the research instrument itself reflects that both are standard practice in this population. I could not retrieve the prevalence figures — [same (snippet)](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12970-018-0209-z)
- Follow-up (Chappell & Simper 2019, JISSN, doi 10.1186/s12970-019-0302-y): higher-placing British natural bodybuilders ran **~3.3 g/kg protein, ~0.6 g/kg fat** and consumed *more* carbohydrate and more total energy than lower-placing peers — [DOI record (snippet)](https://doaj.org/article/d511cf232c8a461b97159d826e269169)
- Authors' own caveat: frequent AAS use in competitive bodybuilding confounds identification of optimal strategies, and the placing/carbohydrate association "requires corroboration" — [same (snippet)](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12970-018-0209-z)
- A review cited alongside reports the broader observed ranges: males **243-637 g/d (3-7.2 g/kg)**, females **160-415 g/d (2.8-7.5 g/kg)**, against published recommendations of 4-7 g/kg for bodybuilders — [secondary review (snippet)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7052702)

**Other case studies identified but not read in full (listed so the writer knows they exist, with what the search returned):**
- Alto et al. 2020, J Strength Cond Res — prospective case study of one high-level natural bodybuilder across **four** competition preps plus post-contest recovery, explicitly assessing a **carbohydrate-deplete-then-load peaking strategy** — [search description only](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35878117/)
- Barakat 2022, Sports (Basel) 10(7):106 — peak-week case study; from the CHO-depleted state 3 days out to show day, **LBM, muscle thickness, total body water and subjective fullness all increased** — [MDPI page (403 for me; search description only)](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/10/7/106)
- Bannock et al. 2024, Frontiers in Nutrition — drug-free bodybuilder, 18 weeks, assessing low-energy-availability markers during chronic restriction plus a **2-day modest energy repletion during peaking** — [Frontiers PDF (search description only)](https://frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1465001/pdf)
- Homer, Cross & Helms 2024, Sports Med-Open — narrative review of **peak-week carbohydrate manipulation in physique athletes** (appeared only in reference lists; findings not retrieved)
- Steen 1991 and Hickson et al. 1990 (IJSNEM) — "Precontest strategies of a male bodybuilder" and a male bodybuilder precontest nutrition paper; cited in reference lists, full text not retrieved

### Inferences
- The *published single-athlete* record and the *interview/survey* record disagree about weekly carbohydrate cycling: the formally supervised case studies (Robinson) ran carbohydrate essentially **flat across training and rest days** and used no refeeds at all, while self-coached and coach-led competitors routinely use a **1 day/week refeed at or near maintenance, surplus mostly carbohydrate**. For the app this means weekly refeeds are **tier (b): common practice, weak evidence** — the single supporting RCT (Campbell) has n=27, 7 weeks, no strength outcome, and a published reanalysis letter disputing its main claim.
- Robinson's 43% FFM-of-loss figure is the single most important practical warning in this literature: an aggressive, low-carb, high-cardio prep at ~1%/wk still cost a trained 86 kg male 5 kg of lean mass. For a 134 kg beginner with >30% BF the same relative rate (1%/wk = 1.34 kg/wk) is far more aggressive in absolute terms than it sounds.
- **134 kg translation of Robinson:** his 100-140 g CHO/d at 86 kg is 1.2-1.6 g/kg total mass, ≈1.35-1.9 g/kg lean mass. Applied to ~92 kg lean mass that is **125-175 g CHO/d** — but he was lifting 4×30 min/wk, not 5-6 sessions/wk. Scaling for 5-6 sessions I would place the floor higher, ~**180-250 g/d**, and treat anything under ~150 g/d on a training day as a red flag for session quality (see glycogen-threshold numbers in Q2).
- **134 kg translation of Campbell's refeed:** the transferable part is the *structure*, not the grams — 5 days at a deeper deficit (he used 35% below maintenance) + 2 consecutive days back at maintenance with the entire added energy as carbohydrate, averaging ~25% deficit for the week. For a 134 kg male whose maintenance is, say, 3,100 kcal: 5 days at ~2,000 kcal and 2 days at ~3,100 kcal, the ~1,100 kcal/day difference = **~275 g extra carbohydrate on each refeed day**. That is a very large single-day carb swing and should be introduced gradually; also note his profile says slow metabolism and calories ~10% under formula, which lowers all of these numbers.
- Chappell's 4-7 g/kg competitor carbohydrate intakes **do not transfer at all** on a total-body-mass basis: 4.1 g/kg × 134 kg = 550 g/d, which is not compatible with a deficit. Expressed per kg lean mass the same 4.1 g/kg gives ~375 g/d, still a maintenance-level figure for someone with no deficit. Flag as **non-transferable without re-basing to lean mass and then capping by the calorie budget**.
- The "higher carbohydrate at the start of prep predicted better placing" finding is cross-sectional and confounded (better athletes start leaner, with more muscle, and can afford more carbohydrate). It is **not** evidence that raising carbohydrate improves outcomes — tier (b) at best.

### Gaps
- No published case study I could reach gives a **day-by-day carbohydrate schedule across a week** for a physique athlete in a deficit. Robinson gives a training day and a rest day; Campbell gives a 5+2 split without the per-day grams. If the report needs a concrete weekly table, it will have to be constructed, not cited.
- Chappell 2018/2019 full texts (refeed prevalence, meal frequency, cardio volume, rate of weight loss) were paywalled (Springer 403, EuropePMC 403). All Chappell numbers above are snippet-derived.
- **No case studies of strength athletes** (powerlifting/weightlifting) with carbohydrate periodisation detail were found — the physique literature dominates. Searches surfaced nothing comparable for strength sport preparation.
- No case study in anyone resembling a 134 kg, >30% BF, untrained-to-intermediate male. Every subject in this literature is a lean trained competitor. This is the single biggest external-validity gap for the app.

## Q2. Endurance and team-sport day-to-day periodisation ("fuel for the work required")

### Takeaway
The "fuel for the work required" framework (Impey/Burke/Morton) deliberately **refuses to publish g/kg day targets** — it is a red/amber/green session-by-session availability framework built around a muscle-glycogen window of roughly **300 down to 100 mmol/kg dw**, with train-low applied in about **30-50% of sessions**. The published g/kg day tables come from the older sport-specific guidelines (3-12 g/kg depending on load; soccer 4-6 g/kg training days, 6-8 g/kg match day and MD-1), and the field data show elite athletes routinely eat *below* those targets.

### Cited Findings

**Impey et al. 2018, Sports Med 48(5):1031-1048 — full text read (PMC version)**
- The paper's Table 3 rates sessions red/amber/green and the authors state they "have **not prescribed specific CHO quantities**" — [PMC5889771](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- Glycogen numbers that define the framework: adaptive signalling responses to CHO restriction are most evident when **pre-exercise glycogen ≤300 mmol/kg dw**; **post-exercise glycogen >500** attenuated exercise-induced gene expression; **post-exercise <100** may reduce regulation of protein-synthesis pathways; **starting exercise below 200 is likely to impair training intensity**. The proposed working window is therefore **~300 → 100 mmol/kg dw** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- In the authors' own exhaustive-capacity test, pre-exercise glycogen 600 (high) vs 300 (low) produced **158 vs 100 minutes** to exhaustion — a ~60-minute performance cost of starting "low" — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- Train-low is proposed for roughly **30-50% of sessions**, applied day-to-day and meal-by-meal rather than as chronic restriction — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- Sleep-low/train-low: CHO fully restricted overnight after the evening session, athlete fed **protein-only** meal or drink; morning session typically started with glycogen **<200**; cumulative low-glycogen period can reach **12-14 h** in sleep-low vs **3-8 h** in twice-daily models. No gram amounts are given — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- Whole-body CHO storage estimated at ~**500 g** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- Amounts used inside the cited trials (protocols, not recommendations): Cochran et al. 2.3 g/kg between twice-daily sessions; Jensen et al. 1 g/kg/h over 4 h recovery; Lane et al. high-CHO comparator diet 4 g/kg/d; Sherman 1981 ~5 g/kg during taper then 8 g/kg for 3 days — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)

**Team-sport (soccer) published day targets — snippet-derived, see caveat**
- Collins et al. 2021 (UEFA expert group), as reproduced in a 2026 IJSNEM review: **MD-1 6-8 g/kg, match day 6-8 g/kg, pre-match meal 1-3 g/kg eaten 3-4 h before kick-off, 30-60 g/h during play** — [DCU repository PDF (snippet)](https://doras.dcu.ie/32533/1/2026%20McManus%20et%20al%20IJSNEM%20Fuelling%20performance%20of%20female%20soccer%20players%2C%20appraisal%20of%20the%20likelihood%20of%20between-sex%20differences%20in%20carbohydrate%20requirements.pdf)
- UEFA guidance as cited in a 2026 LJMU thesis: **4-6 g/kg on training days, 6-8 g/kg on match day** — [LJMU thesis (snippet)](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/28739/1/2026FooPhD.pdf)
- Earlier Burke et al. 2006-style guidance still quoted in soccer papers: **5-7 g/kg on training days, 7-12 g/kg on days requiring enhanced recovery and match preparation** — [Birmingham repository PDF (snippet; page was 403 on direct fetch)](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/222961228/Physical_loading_in_professional_soccer_players_Implications_for_contemporary_guidelines_to_encompass_carbohydrate_periodization.pdf)
- A 2022 consensus-style paper recommends **6-8 g/kg/d over the 24-48 h before a match** — [as cited in the same soccer literature (snippet)](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/222961228/Physical_loading_in_professional_soccer_players_Implications_for_contemporary_guidelines_to_encompass_carbohydrate_periodization.pdf)
- **What elite players actually eat, vs those targets:** English Premier League players consumed only **~4 g/kg on MD-1**; another study found **MD-1 4.6 ± 0.8 and MD+1 3.6 ± 0.5 g/kg/d**, both below guidelines; the literature links MD-1 intakes of 4 g/kg to reduced match-day total distance compared with 8 g/kg — [Anderson et al., LJMU repository (snippet; PDF text not extractable)](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/6323/3/Energy%20Intake%20and%20Expenditure%20of%20Professional%20Soccer%20Players%20of%20the%20English%20Premier%20League%20Evidence%20of%20Carbohydrate%20Periodization.pdf)
- A practitioner summary of the framework notes group mean daily carbohydrate of **3.9 g/kg** in an elite population, well below the 5-10 g/kg usually recommended for 1-3 h/day of training — [Sigma Nutrition, carbohydrate periodization](https://sigmanutrition.com/carbohydrate-periodization/)
- Strength-sport handout figure quoted in sports-nutrition practice materials: **4-7 g/kg/d for strength athletes**, with the explicit admission that amount and timing of carbohydrate before strength training "have not been firmly established" — [GSSI/Gatorade strength handout](https://performancepartner.gatorade.com/content/resources/pdfs/foods-fluids-for-strength-training.pdf)

### Inferences
- The framework's actual operating rule, stripped of g/kg, is: **do not start a session that matters below ~200 mmol/kg dw, do not finish a hard session above ~500, and do not sit below ~100 for long.** That maps cleanly onto app logic as "fuel the hard session, let the easy session run leaner" without needing to model glycogen.
- The 158-vs-100-minute exhaustion result is the honest counterweight to all train-low enthusiasm: **train-low reliably costs work capacity in the session where it is applied.** That is why the framework caps it at 30-50% of sessions and applies it to easy aerobic work, never the key session.
- **134 kg translation:** every g/kg figure in this section (4-12 g/kg) is for endurance/team athletes at near-maintenance energy, and multiplying by 134 gives 536-1,600 g/d — absurd for a lifter in a deficit. These numbers should appear in the app only as *context*, never as targets. The transferable items are: (i) the 1-3 g/kg pre-session meal **3-4 h out** concept, which at 92 kg lean mass is ~90-275 g and in a deficit realistically **60-100 g**; (ii) 30-60 g/h intra-session carbohydrate **only** for sessions beyond ~60-90 min; (iii) the day-type ranking (hard > moderate > easy/off) rather than the absolute values.
- Team-sport practice is a useful precedent for the app's *shape*: carbohydrate is pushed up for the 24-48 h **before** the hardest effort and allowed to fall on the day after. For a lifter the equivalent is placing the high day before or on the hardest session, not after it.
- Note the honest gap between guideline and behaviour: elite professionals eat ~4 g/kg when told 6-8. Any app that prescribes the top of a published range should expect the same adherence failure.

### Gaps
- I could not extract the per-day table from the EPL Anderson et al. paper or the Birmingham soccer paper — both PDFs were unparseable or 403. The MD-by-MD numbers above are snippet-derived and the guideline figures **differ between sources and eras** (5-7/7-12 vs 4-6/6-8), which the writer should flag rather than resolve.
- Impey et al. gives no g/kg targets at all; anyone citing "fuel for the work required daily targets" is importing them from elsewhere. The Burke 2011 "Carbohydrates for training and competition" table (the canonical 3-5 / 5-7 / 6-10 / 8-12 g/kg tiers) was not directly retrieved in this session and should be cited from the original if used.
- No evidence found, in either direction, that train-low/sleep-low protocols benefit **hypertrophy or strength** outcomes. The entire framework is built on endurance/mitochondrial adaptation.

## Q3. What named coaches publish as their own systems

### Takeaway
The only coach system I could verify from a **primary, peer-reviewed** source is Helms/Aragon/Fitschen (JISSN 2014), and notably it prescribes **protein, fat and rate of loss numerically but leaves carbohydrate as the residual** — with a single explicit adjustment rule (+25-50 g CHO near contest leanness). Everything I could find on Israetel/RP, and nothing at all on Norton, Nippard or Henselmans, came through secondary blog write-ups; those are flagged and should not be reported as the coaches' own published positions.

### Cited Findings

**Helms, Aragon & Fitschen 2014, JISSN 11:20 — full text read; this is the closest thing to a published coach system**
- Rate of loss: **~0.5-1% of bodyweight per week**, stated as best for muscle retention; a 500 kcal/d deficit theoretically yields ~0.5 kg/wk if all loss were fat — [PMC4033492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- Protein: **2.3-3.1 g/kg of lean body mass per day** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- Fat: **15-30% of total calories**, with 15-20% acceptable if higher fat would push protein or carbohydrate below ideal — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- **Carbohydrate: deliberately not given as a fixed g/kg for contest prep.** The paper cites the generic 4-7 g/kg/d for strength sports but states a prep deficit likely cannot accommodate the high end; carbohydrate is **whatever remains after protein and fat** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- The one explicit carbohydrate adjustment rule: once at or near competition leanness, **carbohydrate can be increased by 25-50 g, reducing the deficit by ~100-200 kcal** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- Starting point: a 70 kg athlete at 13% BF should be **no more than 6-7 kg above contest weight** when prep begins — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- Meal frequency: **3-6 meals/day**, each ≥~20 g protein, with 30-40 g high-quality protein per meal suggested to reach the leucine threshold and 3-4 meals to distribute daily protein — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- Nutrient timing: **0.4-0.5 g/kg bodyweight protein in a meal before and after resistance training**; **no specific pre/post-workout carbohydrate amount is recommended for typical resistance sessions**; only for sessions ~≥2 h do they recommend 8-15 g protein with **30-60 g carbohydrate per hour as a 6-8% solution** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- Carbohydrate loading: **no numeric protocol given**; any load should be trialled before competition, once at or near contest leanness, and a trial week may slow fat loss — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- **Refeeds, diet breaks and "full days of eating" are not quantified anywhere in the paper** — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)

### Inferences
- The most striking, and most app-relevant, fact in this section is **what the published coach system does not do**: Helms/Aragon/Fitschen set protein per kg **lean** mass, set fat as a percentage, set a rate of loss, and then let carbohydrate be the remainder. An app built on that logic needs protein and fat rules plus a calorie target; carbohydrate falls out automatically and never needs its own target. That is a cleaner architecture than a carb-first design.
- Their one adjustment rule (**+25-50 g carbohydrate ≈ −100-200 kcal of deficit when leanness is reached**) is directly implementable as the app's step size for easing a deficit. For a 134 kg male the same absolute step is proportionally smaller, so a slightly larger step (**+40-60 g**) is defensible, but the principle — adjust in one 100-200 kcal increment at a time, delivered as carbohydrate, and judge from the scale over 1-2 weeks — transfers unchanged.
- The lean-mass basis matters enormously here: 2.3-3.1 g/kg **LBM** protein at ~92 kg LBM = **210-285 g protein/d** for the 134 kg user, versus 310-415 g if someone wrongly used total bodyweight. Any protein/carb split the app computes must be lean-mass-based.
- Helms' refusal to recommend pre/post-workout carbohydrate for normal resistance sessions, combined with the 30-60 g/h rule only beyond ~2 h, means **intra-workout carbohydrate is tier (c)/folklore for a 60-90 min lifting session** and the app should not suggest it.
- RP's "1/8 of daily calories pre-workout, 1:3 protein:carb, 60-70 g carbs" is internally consistent with a ~4,000 kcal maintenance lifter; at a 134 kg male's deficit calories, 1/8 of intake is ~250-300 kcal, so the same rule yields **~40-50 g carbohydrate pre-session**, not 60-70. Flag as a rule that must be scaled by intake, not copied.

## Q4. Where practitioners and the research literature disagree

### Takeaway
Three live disagreements: (1) whether weekly carbohydrate cycling/refeeds do anything beyond matching calories — mainstream nutrition science says no, physique practice says yes for adherence and lean-mass retention, and the one supporting RCT has a published rebuttal letter; (2) whether diet breaks preserve lean mass — trials say mainly psychological benefit, practice claims metabolic benefit; (3) whether train-low belongs anywhere near resistance training — the framework's authors never claimed it does, but the idea has leaked into lifting practice.

### Cited Findings
- **Calories-matched position:** Harvard Health states the evidence for carb cycling for weight loss is "even thinner" than for performance, and that research consistently shows low-carb diets are not more effective for weight loss than higher-carb diets when total calories are equal — [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/diet-and-nutrition/carb-cycling-what-is-it-and-does-it-work)
- WeightWatchers' review states there is currently **no scientific data linking carb cycling to faster-than-average weight loss** — [WeightWatchers](https://www.weightwatchers.com/au/blog/weight-loss/carb-cycling)
- Scale-weight artefact: some apparent loss during carb cycling is **water weight**, not fat — [MyFitnessPal](https://blog.myfitnesspal.com/what-is-carb-cycling-and-does-it-promote-weight-loss/)
- Mechanistic criticism: the theory that low-carb days increase fat oxidation does not automatically mean more fat loss overall — [ZOE](https://zoe.com/learn/what-is-carb-cycling-does-it-work)
- **The pro-refeed trial:** Campbell et al. 2020 reported FFM, dry FFM and RMR preserved with a 2-day carbohydrate refeed vs continuous restriction, with fat loss not significantly different — [PMC7739314](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- **Its published rebuttal:** a letter attached to that article argues that "only dry fat-free mass was different between groups upon reanalysis" — [same article page](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- **Diet breaks, ICECAP secondary analysis (Peos et al. 2021, PLoS ONE):** in 26 resistance-trained athletes, a 1-week diet break after 12 weeks of intermittent restriction had **no significant effect on fat mass**, produced small but significant rises in body weight (**+0.6 kg**), fat-free mass (**+0.7 kg**) and resting energy expenditure, improved **leg muscle endurance but not strength**, and reduced hunger and irritability while increasing alertness — [PMC7906362](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362)
- Peos et al. 2019 review: evidence for the effectiveness of intermittent energy restriction **in athletic populations is lacking**; their guidance is explicitly cautious and theory-based — [PubMed 30373624-era review, via search](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467235/)
- A 2023 RCT in 38 resistance-trained women found diet breaks **not superior** to continuous restriction for body composition or metabolic rate over 6 weeks, though they may suit people wanting a short-term break — [PMC listing, search description only; not read in full](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10170537)
- **Train-low's own cost, from its proponents:** Impey et al. report 158 vs 100 min exercise capacity at high vs low glycogen, and warn that starting below 200 mmol/kg dw likely impairs training intensity — i.e. the framework's authors concede the performance penalty and bound it to 30-50% of sessions — [PMC5889771](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- Practice outruns guidance in the opposite direction too: elite footballers eat **~4-4.6 g/kg on MD-1** when guidelines say 6-8, and the same literature associates 4 g/kg with reduced match-day distance vs 8 g/kg — [LJMU/Anderson (snippet)](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/6323/3/Energy%20Intake%20and%20Expenditure%20of%20Professional%20Soccer%20Players%20of%20the%20English%20Premier%20League%20Evidence%20of%20Carbohydrate%20Periodization.pdf)
- Bodybuilding practice is itself drifting toward the evidence on one point: **4 of 7 interviewed natural bodybuilders had abandoned the classic 3-day depletion + 3-day load, and 6 of 7 now use a modified load** — [PMC5969027](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/)

### Inferences
- The honest reading for the app: **carbohydrate cycling does not beat calorie-matched steady dieting for fat loss (tier a: the null is reasonably well supported).** Its defensible claims are adherence, training quality on hard days, and possibly lean-mass retention (tier b, one contested RCT). The app should sell it as a *scheduling and adherence tool*, not a fat-loss mechanism — that framing is both more honest and harder to falsify.
- Diet breaks have the clearest evidence for the thing practitioners talk about least: **hunger, irritability and alertness** (tier a from ICECAP's secondary analysis), while the metabolic/lean-mass claims practitioners lead with are weak or null (tier b). Also note the +0.6 kg weight and +0.7 kg FFM after a 1-week break are substantially glycogen and water — an app must pre-warn users that the scale will rise on and after a refeed or break, or it will cause panic and bad adjustment decisions.
- **Train-low applied to lifting is tier (c) folklore.** Nothing in the source framework supports it, the framework's own data show a large capacity cost, and the glycogen floor (<100 mmol/kg dw possibly reducing protein-synthesis signalling) argues directly against it for a hypertrophy goal.
- The genuine argument on the practitioners' side is selection: trials are 6-7 weeks in modestly dieting subjects, while coaches work with 16-24 week preps in much leaner athletes where psychological collapse, not metabolism, ends the diet. "No difference at 7 weeks in people at 20% body fat" does not settle "useful at week 18 of a prep at 7% body fat." That asymmetry should be stated rather than resolved.
- For a 134 kg male at >30% BF, the practitioner argument is at its **weakest**: he is nowhere near the leanness at which refeeds and diet breaks are claimed to earn their keep, and he has the largest available fat stores and the most room for a simple consistent deficit. Carb cycling for him is justified by *behaviour* (a planned high day that replaces unplanned overeating) and by *training quality* (fuelling the hardest sessions), not by physiology.

### Gaps
- I did not find the named coaches' own published rebuttals of the null findings, so the "practitioner side" above is reconstructed from survey/interview data and from the structure of the trials, not quoted from Helms/Norton/Israetel/Henselmans. The writer should not attribute these arguments to individuals.
- The 2023 female diet-break RCT and the Campbell rebuttal letter were identified but not read in full; their exact numbers should be verified.
- No meta-analysis of carbohydrate cycling for body composition was located. The field appears not to have one, which is itself worth stating.

## Q5. Practical details that matter for an app: training time of day, aerobic vs heavy lift, placing high days

### Takeaway
The transferable practical rules are few and simple: fuel the **hardest** session and the 24 h before it, run the easy aerobic session leaner (and only ever the easy one), put the pre-session carbohydrate meal **3-4 h out** or shrink and move it closer when training early, and keep carbohydrate away from being the thing that gets cut on heavy days. Beyond that, the published detail thins out fast and much of what is circulated is practice without trial support.

### Cited Findings
- **Pre-session meal timing and size (team sport, published guideline):** 1-3 g/kg carbohydrate **3-4 h before** the key effort — [Collins et al. 2021 as reproduced in a 2026 IJSNEM review (snippet)](https://doras.dcu.ie/32533/1/2026%20McManus%20et%20al%20IJSNEM%20Fuelling%20performance%20of%20female%20soccer%20players%2C%20appraisal%20of%20the%20likelihood%20of%20between-sex%20differences%20in%20carbohydrate%20requirements.pdf)
- **During-session carbohydrate:** 30-60 g/h during match play — [same (snippet)](https://doras.dcu.ie/32533/1/2026%20McManus%20et%20al%20IJSNEM%20Fuelling%20performance%20of%20female%20soccer%20players%2C%20appraisal%20of%20the%20likelihood%20of%20between-sex%20differences%20in%20carbohydrate%20requirements.pdf); for resistance training, Helms/Aragon/Fitschen recommend it **only for sessions ~≥2 h**, as 8-15 g protein + 30-60 g carbohydrate per hour in a 6-8% solution — [PMC4033492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- **No pre/post carbohydrate prescription for ordinary lifting:** the same paper gives protein peri-workout (0.4-0.5 g/kg before and after) but explicitly no carbohydrate amount for typical resistance sessions — [PMC4033492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- **Meal structure in a real prep:** 4 meals + 1 snack/day, with a deliberate **late-evening low-carbohydrate, high-protein snack**, and fasted cardio placed at **07:00-08:00** separate from the lifting session — [PMC4422265](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422265)
- **Hard-session-first placement, team-sport version:** carbohydrate is raised across the **24-48 h before** the hardest effort (MD-1 and match day at 6-8 g/kg) and allowed to fall on the day after (MD+1 observed at 3.6 g/kg) — [guidelines (snippet)](https://doras.dcu.ie/32533/1/2026%20McManus%20et%20al%20IJSNEM%20Fuelling%20performance%20of%20female%20soccer%20players%2C%20appraisal%20of%20the%20likelihood%20of%20between-sex%20differences%20in%20carbohydrate%20requirements.pdf); [observed intakes (snippet)](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/6323/3/Energy%20Intake%20and%20Expenditure%20of%20Professional%20Soccer%20Players%20of%20the%20English%20Premier%20League%20Evidence%20of%20Carbohydrate%20Periodization.pdf)
- **Which sessions may run low:** train-low is for 30-50% of sessions, and starting below ~200 mmol/kg dw impairs intensity — so the low sessions must be the ones where intensity does not matter — [PMC5889771](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- **Sleep-low mechanics (the only published protocol that explicitly links evening eating to next-morning session quality):** evening session, then overnight carbohydrate fully restricted with a protein-only feed, morning session begun at glycogen <200, total low window 12-14 h — [PMC5889771](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889771)
- **Where the weekly refeed sits in practice:** interviewed natural bodybuilders used **one refeed day per week**, placed by the athlete; Campbell's trial placed the two refeed days **typically Saturday-Sunday**, i.e. at the end of the training week rather than before the hardest session — [PMC5969027](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969027/); [PMC7739314](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739314)
- **Meal frequency bounds from the published coach system:** 3-6 meals/day, ≥20 g protein each, 30-40 g/meal suggested for the leucine threshold — [PMC4033492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/)
- Israetel's practice position on frequency (secondary source): four or more meals every 3-6 h, with total protein mattering more than per-meal precision — [BarBend](https://barbend.com/news/what-aspects-of-nutrient-timing-matter-most/)

### Inferences
Rules I would consider safe to implement for the 134 kg user, with tiers:

- **Place the high/refeed day on, or the day before, the hardest session** — tier (b). The evidence base for *placement* is team-sport practice (load the 24-48 h before the match) plus Impey's glycogen logic; note that the one RCT placed refeeds on the weekend for convenience, not physiology, so placement is practice, not trial-supported.
- **Pre-lift carbohydrate meal 3-4 h out when the schedule allows; when training early morning, shrink it and move it to ~60-90 min out** — tier (b). The 3-4 h / 1-3 g/kg figure is published guideline; the early-morning compression is standard practice without a trial I could cite. At the user's deficit calories, ~40-80 g carbohydrate in that meal is a realistic figure (derived, not cited).
- **A long easy aerobic session is the one session that can be fuelled lightly or fasted; a heavy lift is not** — tier (a) for the principle that low glycogen impairs intensity (Impey's 158 vs 100 min and the <200 warning), tier (b) for the specific practice. Robinson's prep did exactly this: fasted cardio at 07:00-08:00, carbohydrate placed around lifting.
- **Carbohydrate around the lift is for session quality, not for a post-workout "window"** — tier (a) on the negative claim: the published coach system gives no peri-workout carbohydrate number for ordinary resistance sessions, so insisting on fast carbs immediately post-lift is tier (c).
- **Intra-workout carbohydrate only beyond ~90-120 min of training** — tier (a)/(b): 30-60 g/h is well supported in endurance/team sport; its application to lifting is bounded by Helms' ~2 h threshold. For a 60-90 min lifting session it is folklore.
- **Keep a protein-dominant, low-carbohydrate evening meal on low days** — tier (b). Both the real prep (Robinson's late-evening low-carb high-protein snack) and the sleep-low protocol use evening carbohydrate restriction, but for different goals; for a lifter the justification is calorie control and satiety, not adaptation.
- **4-5 meals/day, each with 30-40 g protein minimum** — tier (a) for the protein distribution rationale, tier (b) for the exact meal count. At 210-285 g protein/day (2.3-3.1 g/kg of ~92 kg LBM) this lands at ~45-60 g protein per meal across 4-5 meals, which the user's existing 4-meal maximum accommodates.
- **Adjustment step size: one change at a time of 25-50 g carbohydrate (~100-200 kcal), judged over 1-2 weeks from scale trend** — tier (b), taken directly from the published coach system's one explicit rule and from the fact that an app cannot measure glycogen or RMR.
- **Warn before every refeed/high day that scale weight will rise 0.5-1.5 kg and take 2-4 days to settle** — tier (a) on the mechanism (ICECAP's +0.6 kg over a 1-week break; the carb-cycling water-weight artefact). This is the highest-value piece of coaching copy in the whole file for an automated app, because the alternative is the user cutting calories in response to glycogen water.

Non-transfer flags for this user:
- Every g/kg total-bodyweight target in this literature (4-7 g/kg bodybuilders, 4-12 g/kg endurance/team) **must be re-based to lean mass** or it produces 500-1,600 g/day figures.
- Fasted early-morning cardio as used in Robinson's prep was in a lean 86 kg trained athlete; for a 134 kg male with knee-limiting jumps, the session type matters more than the fasted/fed question, and his profile already rules out running.
- Peak-week depletion/loading protocols (3-day deplete + 3-day load, and even the "modified" versions) are **competition-stage tools with no relevance to this user** and should not appear in the app.

### Gaps
- **No published source I could reach addresses training time of day and carbohydrate placement directly for resistance training.** The 3-4 h pre-meal figure comes from soccer match preparation. Early-morning-lifter guidance in practice circulates without trial support, and I did not find a citable practitioner source for it in this session.
- No source quantifies how much carbohydrate a "long easy aerobic session" should get for a lifter in a deficit; the endurance numbers assume maintenance energy.
- No source gives a validated decision algorithm (if-stall-then-adjust) for automated coaching. Everything in the adjustment-rule inference above is extrapolated from Helms' single +25-50 g rule.
- Peak-week numbers from Barakat 2022, Alto 2020 and Bannock 2024 were all blocked (403/PDF); if the report wants a documented day-by-day carbohydrate schedule with real grams, those three are the papers to obtain.
