# Refeeds, Diet Breaks, Protein Requirements and Metabolic Adaptation During Prolonged Fat Loss

Scope note: subject is a 23-year-old male, ~134 kg, >30% body fat, lifting 5–6 d/week, on semaglutide, in a deficit, target ~105 kg. Almost all refeed/diet-break trial evidence comes from *lean, resistance-trained* samples (~21% body fat, ~73 kg) or from *untrained men with obesity*. Neither population matches the subject exactly, and no trial has tested refeeds or diet breaks in people on GLP-1 therapy. This is flagged throughout.

---

## Q1. Refeeds (1–2 high-carbohydrate days per week): what do controlled trials show?

### Takeaway
There is exactly one RCT of weekly carbohydrate refeeds in resistance-trained dieters (Campbell et al. 2020), its headline claim of preserved fat-free mass and RMR did not survive reanalysis, and the only remaining significant finding (dry fat-free mass, +0.9 kg) rests on a hydration-sensitive BIA-derived estimate in n=27. Refeeds should be encoded as an **adherence/performance tool with no demonstrated body-composition benefit**, not as a metabolic intervention.

### Cited Findings
- Campbell et al. (2020, *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 5(1):19) randomised 27 resistance-trained males and females to ~25% energy restriction for 7 weeks with either **two consecutive higher-carbohydrate (refeed) days per week** plus five restricted days, or continuous daily restriction, both with resistance training. The authors concluded the refeed approach preserved fat-free mass, dry fat-free mass and resting metabolic rate — [MDPI JFMK 5(1):19](https://www.mdpi.com/2411-5142/5/1/19)
- A published comment/reanalysis in the same journal reported that, **contrary to the original conclusion, there was no significant between-group difference in change in FFM or in RMR**; the only significant difference was dry FFM, greater in the refeed group by **0.9 kg (95% CI 0.33 to 1.46; p = 0.0028)**, and the comment recommended the original conclusions be corrected to reflect that only one of the three outcomes differed — [JFMK comment/reanalysis (PMC7739336)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739336)
- The dry-FFM result was described as robust to completers-only ANCOVA and completers-only Welch's t-test on change scores in sensitivity checks — [JFMK comment/reanalysis (PMC7739336)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739336)
- The original authors published a reply stating that **changes in fat-free mass and RMR were not significantly different between the two diet treatments**, while noting both were numerically better maintained in the refeed group — [JFMK reply, 5(4) (PMC7739255)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739255)
- Methodological critique: the group × time interaction was significant only for dry FFM, not total FFM or RMR, and dry-mass estimates derived from bioelectrical impedance are unreliable because carbohydrate refeeding itself shifts hydration/glycogen — [Henselmans review of Campbell et al.](https://mennohenselmans.com/campbell-refeed-study-review/) (secondary/opinion source; the author discloses being a friend of the lead author)
- The best-controlled "carbohydrate refeed" data are actually from the ICECAP diet-break analysis, where the entire energy increase came from carbohydrate (fat held constant). Over one week at maintenance, **leptin did not change significantly (1910 → 2100 pg/ml, P = 0.894)**, **free T3 did not change significantly (3.79 → 4.01 pmol/L, P = 0.120)**, testosterone 2.13 → 2.28 ng/ml (P = 0.906), IGF-1 183 → 196 ng/ml (P = 0.106), active ghrelin 141 → 155 pg/ml (P = 0.647), total PYY 93.1 → 91.5 pg/ml (P = 0.830) — all n = 13 — [Peos et al. 2021, PLoS One 16(2):e0247292](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362/)
- In the same analysis, **resting energy expenditure rose only from ~7000 to ~7200 kJ/day (~1670 → ~1720 kcal/day; P = 0.026)** — a ~50 kcal/day change, and body weight rose 0.6 kg and FFM 0.7 kg (both P < 0.001) while **fat mass did not change (15.2 → 15.1 kg, P = 0.278)** — [Peos et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362/)
- What carbohydrate refeeding *did* do measurably: **leg muscle endurance improved** (hamstring total work 1280 → 1380 J, P = 0.018; quadriceps total work 2530 → 2660 J, P = 0.018), while **strength (3-rep peak torque/power, arms and legs) did not change**, and arm endurance did not change — [Peos et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362/)
- Appetite and mood improved substantially: hunger 34.8 → 19.2 (P = 0.017), prospective consumption 54.3 → 41.0 (P = 0.020), irritability 21.6 → 10.7 (P = 0.041), fullness 41.5 → 56.1 (P = 0.002), satisfaction 53.5 → 69.6 (P = 0.002), alertness 47.1 → 66.6 (P = 0.003), on 0–100 scales, n = 22 — [Peos et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362/)

### Inferences
- The leptin null result is the single most important finding for app logic: the popular rationale for refeeds ("restore leptin, restore thyroid, restore metabolic rate") was directly tested and failed. A rule that promises hormonal restoration from a refeed would be unsupported.
- The FFM "gain" of 0.7 kg over one week of carbohydrate loading is almost certainly glycogen plus associated water, not tissue, since fat mass was unchanged and the time frame is one week. Any app that displays FFM change around a refeed must suppress or annotate the reading, or the user will misinterpret a 0.6–1.0 kg jump.
- The ~50 kcal/day REE rise is statistically significant but practically trivial (<3% of REE) and roughly equal to a rounding error in a TDEE estimate. Refeeds should not trigger an upward recalculation of maintenance calories.
- The defensible mechanism is **muscle glycogen → training work capacity**, plus appetite/mood relief. These are legitimate reasons to schedule refeeds, and they are the ones to surface in-app.
- Because carbohydrate refeeds raise body water, a refeed day placed immediately before a weigh-in will corrupt a trend-weight-based calorie adjustment algorithm. Refeeds should be placed at a fixed point in the week and the algorithm should use a 7-day rolling average spanning a full cycle.

### Gaps
- No refeed RCT exists in people with obesity, in people on GLP-1 therapy, or with DXA plus a deuterium/4-compartment criterion method rather than BIA.
- No study has compared 1 vs 2 refeed days per week, or compared refeed magnitudes.
- No newer meta-analysis specific to weekly refeeds in trained dieters was found; the Campbell trial appears to still be the only RCT of that design.
- I could not independently verify the exact author list/volume of the JFMK reanalysis comment; it is cited here by its PMC record and content.

## Q2. Diet breaks (1–2 weeks at maintenance): effect size, who benefits, how often, evidence quality

### Takeaway
MATADOR (untrained men with obesity, 2-weeks-on/2-weeks-off) is the only trial showing a large benefit (−14.1 vs −9.1 kg), but its intermittent arm ran 30 weeks against 16, so the comparison is confounded by elapsed time; ICECAP (trained athletes, 3-weeks-on/1-week-off) found **no difference in fat loss or FFM retention** and only an appetite benefit. Diet breaks are best encoded as an **optional adherence/recovery lever**, with the MATADOR effect flagged as unreplicated and population-specific.

### Cited Findings
**MATADOR (Byrne et al., Int J Obes 2018) — men with obesity, the strongest pro-break result**
- Design: single-centre parallel-group RCT, 51 men randomised (CON n=25, INT n=26), aged 25–54, BMI 30–45. CON = 16 weeks continuous restriction. INT = **8 × 2-week restriction blocks alternating with 7 × 2-week energy-balance blocks, 30 weeks total**. Restriction was **67% of maintenance (a 33% deficit) in both arms**; energy-balance blocks at 100% of maintenance. Meals were provided. Planned macros: 25–30% fat, **15–20% protein**, 50–60% carbohydrate — [MATADOR, PMC5803575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Weight at end of the 16 weeks of restriction (per protocol, CON n=19 / INT n=17): **CON −9.2 ± 3.7 kg (−8.4%) vs INT −14.1 ± 5.6 kg (−12.9%); difference 4.8 kg, P = 0.004** — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Fat mass: **CON −8.0 ± 4.4 kg vs INT −12.3 ± 4.8 kg; difference 4.3 kg, P = 0.009**; fat was ~87% of weight lost. Weight change during INT energy-balance blocks averaged **0.0 ± 0.3 kg** — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Fat-free mass: **CON −1.2 ± 2.4 kg vs INT −1.8 ± 1.6 kg; difference 0.6 kg, P = 0.42 (not significant)** — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Resting energy expenditure: absolute change CON −624 ± 557 vs INT −502 ± 481 kJ/d (**P = 0.48, NS**). Adjusted for fat and fat-free mass: **CON −749 ± 498 vs INT −360 ± 502 kJ/d, P = 0.03** (~9% vs ~4% reduction) — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- 6-month free-living follow-up (subsample CON n=13, INT n=15): regain was not significantly different (INT 3.5 ± 5.9 vs CON 5.9 ± 4.7 kg, P = 0.24), but **net loss from baseline was INT −11.1 ± 7.4 vs CON −3.0 ± 4.4 kg, P = 0.001**; fat mass remained significantly below baseline only in INT — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Stated limitations: substantial attrition (51 randomised → 36 per protocol), no blinding, men only, single centre, energy-balance blocks were tightly controlled rather than free-living, REE and body composition were **not measured during INT energy-balance blocks**, food diaries were collected but not analysed for adherence, only the 2:2-week block design was tested, and the authors describe the findings as preliminary — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Leptin and thyroid hormones were **not reported** in MATADOR — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)

**ICECAP (Peos et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2021) — resistance-trained athletes, the null result**
- Parent trial: 61 enrolled, 30 randomised to the intermittent arm (26 reached the diet break). Intermittent arm alternated **3-week blocks of moderate restriction with 1-week diet breaks over 15 weeks (12 weeks of actual restriction)**. Target rate **~0.7% of body weight per week**. **Protein target 2.3 g/kg body weight/day throughout**, fat ~20% of energy, remainder carbohydrate. Over the 12 restricted weeks participants lost **~4.2 kg and 3.6% body fat**. Registration ACTRN12618000638235 — [Peos et al. 2021, PLoS One (describing the parent trial)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362/)
- Main trial result: **moderate intermittent energy restriction did not produce lower fat mass or body weight, nor greater fat-free mass, than continuous restriction** after the restriction period; the authors concluded intermittent restriction **is associated with reduced appetite** — [ICECAP, PubMed 33467300](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467300/) (retrieved via search summary; see Gaps)
- Pre-specified secondary analysis of the 1-week break (n = 26, mean age 29.3 ± 6.4 y, 72.7 kg, 21.3% body fat, 15 M / 11 F): one week at ~100% of maintenance, extra energy entirely from carbohydrate, self-reported intake 6950 → 8720 kJ/day (+~1770 kJ/day, P < 0.001). Outcomes as listed in Q1 — small weight/FFM gain, no fat-mass change, +~50 kcal/day REE, no hormonal change, leg endurance up, strength unchanged, appetite and mood improved. **No independent control group**, blood samples from only 13 participants, and the authors state results should be interpreted with caution — [Peos et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362/)
- Independent critique: the diet breaks "didn't provide any significant benefits hormonally," and there were no between-group differences in physical activity, reported energy intake, or diet adherence; the reviewer's conclusion is that someone who feels they need diet breaks is probably better off simply dieting more slowly — [Henselmans ICECAP review](https://mennohenselmans.com/icecap-diet-break-study-review/) (opinion source; page returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch, so quoted via search snippet)

**Meta-analyses of intermittent vs continuous energy restriction**
- Cioffi et al. (2018, *J Transl Med*): intermittent restriction produced **comparable weight loss (WMD −0.61 kg; 95% CI −1.70 to 0.47)** vs continuous; across 11 trials **no between-arm differences in the other variables**, the only exception being a slight reduction in fasting insulin — [Cioffi et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12967-018-1748-4)
- Xu et al. (2023, *Frontiers in Nutrition*): 16 articles / 20 trials, **1,511 participants** with metabolic syndrome; reports intermittent restriction more effective for body weight, body fat **and fat-free mass**, with no differences in other outcomes. PROSPERO CRD42023397188. These were **secondary outcomes** in a metabolic-syndrome population — [Xu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1090792)

### Inferences
- The MATADOR vs ICECAP divergence tracks population and deficit size: MATADOR used a 33% deficit in untrained men with obesity (where compensatory adaptation has more room to operate), ICECAP used ~0.7%/week in lean trained athletes with 2.3 g/kg protein. The subject is closer to the MATADOR population in body composition but closer to ICECAP in training status and (likely) protein intake, so the expected benefit sits between the two — i.e. probably small.
- The single biggest confound in MATADOR is calendar time: 30 weeks (INT) vs 16 weeks (CON) to accumulate the same 16 weeks of restriction. Part of the "efficiency" gain is simply that the intermittent arm spent half as much of each month in a deficit, so each restricted week was done at a smaller cumulative adaptation. This is a real-world benefit but it is not free — the same endpoint takes roughly twice as long.
- MATADOR's REE finding (significant only after adjusting for body composition, NS in absolute terms) means diet breaks blunted the *adaptive* component (~5 percentage points, ~390 kJ/d ≈ ~93 kcal/day) but not total REE decline. That magnitude is below the noise floor of consumer calorie tracking.
- For app encoding: a 1-week maintenance break is cheap (cost ≈ one week of foregone loss, ~1 kg at the subject's rate) and reliably improves hunger, mood, alertness and leg work capacity. That is a defensible trigger, but the trigger should be **symptom-based** (hunger/irritability/training performance/trend-weight stall), not calendar-fixed, since the hormonal rationale for fixed scheduling does not hold.
- A 2-week break (MATADOR) is the only break length with evidence of a body-composition advantage; a 1-week break (ICECAP) has evidence only for appetite and endurance. If the app offers one break length for a fat-loss benefit claim, 2 weeks is the length with the supporting data, and the claim must be hedged.

### Gaps
- Direct retrieval of the ICECAP main-trial abstract failed (PubMed required cookies, EuropePMC returned HTTP 403, Henselmans 403). ICECAP's main numeric results here come from search summaries plus the parent-trial description inside the Peos 2021 PLoS One secondary analysis. **Exact ICECAP between-group kg values, CIs and p-values were not verified and should be confirmed before being written into app copy.**
- MATADOR 2 (females, registered ANZCTR) results were not located; I found only the registration.
- No trial has compared diet-break frequency (e.g. every 3 weeks vs every 6 weeks vs symptom-triggered), and no trial has tested diet breaks during GLP-1 therapy.
- Xu et al. 2023 full-text effect sizes for fat-free mass were not retrieved, so the magnitude of that reported FFM advantage is unknown and it conflicts with Cioffi 2018.

## Q3. Protein: g/kg during an aggressive deficit for a person with obesity who lifts

### Takeaway
The 2.3–3.1 g/kg **fat-free mass** range is the best-supported target for trained dieters but was derived from lean athletes; for a 134 kg man at >30% body fat it is the *denominator*, not the number, that decides the recommendation — and the three common denominators (total mass at ~1.6 g/kg, FFM at ~2.3 g/kg, target weight at ~2.0 g/kg) converge on roughly **200–230 g/day**, which is the practical target. Clinical GLP-1 guidance is far lower (1.2–1.6 g/kg actual weight) and the real-world problem on semaglutide is reaching any of these numbers at all.

### Cited Findings
**Trained-population targets (per kg fat-free mass)**
- Helms et al. (2014, *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab*): protein needs for energy-restricted resistance-trained athletes are likely **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass**, scaled upward with severity of caloric restriction and leanness — [PubMed 24092765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/)
- Refalo et al. systematic review with meta-regression: **>97% probability of a linear dose-response relationship between daily protein intake and favourable fat-free mass changes**, expressed per kg of fat-free mass; the review also notes the original 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM recommendation rests on limited data — [Refalo et al. (AUT repository)](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/1aa54baf-6885-4739-aba7-df0087c3eae8/full)
- Athlete-focused review: athletes aiming to reduce fat mass and preserve FFM should consume **~1.8–2.7 g/kg body weight/day (or ~2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM)**, combined with a moderate energy deficit (~−500 kcal) and some form of resistance exercise — [Hector/Phillips-style review, PubMed 25014731](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25014731/)
- Counterpoint: some work argues intakes above ~1.6 g/kg/day add little for trained people, and **1.7 g/kg was shown sufficient during a 2-week 40% energy restriction** — [cited within Refalo review](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/1aa54baf-6885-4739-aba7-df0087c3eae8/full)

**Obesity-specific**
- Higher-protein diets are associated with greater fat loss and greater retention of lean mass during energy restriction in individuals with obesity — [review (openrepository.aut.ac.nz)](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content)
- A review of weight-loss strategies and skeletal muscle loss supports combining resistance and aerobic exercise to preserve fat-free mass and argues protein intake above the 0.8 g/kg/day guideline belongs in weight-management prescriptions — [PubMed 34371981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34371981/)
- **No review was found that specifies a g/kg-fat-free-mass protein target derived from a population with obesity**; applying the 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM figure to people with obesity is an extrapolation from lean athletes.

**Per-meal distribution and leucine**
- ISSN 2017 position stand (Jäger et al.): per-serving benchmark of **0.25 g/kg of high-quality protein or an absolute 20–40 g**, with acute doses containing **700–3000 mg leucine**, ideally **evenly distributed every 3–4 h** across the day — [ISSN protein & exercise position stand](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)
- Schoenfeld & Aragon (2018, *JISSN* — a review, **not** a position stand): to maximise anabolism, target **0.4 g/kg/meal across a minimum of four meals** to reach a minimum of 1.6 g/kg/day; using an upper daily intake of 2.2 g/kg/day over the same four meals would require **up to 0.55 g/kg/meal**. The review argues the commonly cited 20–25 g per-meal ceiling is not a hard limit — [PubMed 29497353](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/)
- GLP-1-specific per-meal guidance (Arslan 2026, as summarised by GSSI): **~0.3–0.4 g/kg adjusted body weight per meal, with roughly 2.5–3.0 g leucine per meal** — [GSSI Sports Science Exchange: GLP-1 nutrition optimisation](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)

**Clinical GLP-1 protein guidance (much lower than the physique literature)**
- Mozaffarian et al. (2025): **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day** during active weight loss to help preserve muscle — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)
- Sievenpiper et al. (2025): **1.2–1.5 g/kg actual body weight/day** during weight loss, or 25–30% of energy on a 1,600 kcal diet; ≥0.8 g/kg/day during maintenance; ≥1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for older adults, those at risk of sarcopenia, and the highly active — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)
- Mogna-Peláez & Guasch-Ferré: **up to 1.6–2.0 g/kg/day** for highly active individuals; Gigliotti et al. (2025) set a floor of **60 g/day** — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)
- The GSSI review explicitly states there is **no consensus on whether to calculate needs from actual, ideal, or adjusted body weight** — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)
- Real-world shortfall on GLP-1 therapy (Johnson et al. 2025c): mean protein intake **77.3 g/day** and fibre 14.5 g/day; **only 43% met 1.2 g/kg/day, 10% met 1.6 g/kg/day, and 5% met 2.0 g/kg/day** — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)
- GLP-1 users' intakes fell below the DRI for vitamin D, iron, calcium, choline, magnesium, potassium, and vitamins A, C and E; micronutrient deficiency was documented in **12.7% of patients by 6 months and 22.4% by 12 months** (vitamin D most common: 7.5% and 13.6%) (Scott Butsch et al. 2025), and iron absorption fell ~13% after 10 weeks of semaglutide (Melis et al. 2025) — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)
- Dehydration is the most frequent adverse event contributing to serious outcomes on these drugs (He et al. 2024) — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)
- Semaglutide reduces energy intake by ~24% at 1.0 mg (12 weeks) and ~35% at 2.4 mg (20 weeks); in a 60-week trial the reduction was 30.1% at week 20, 25.4% at week 40 and 21.9% at week 60 — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies)

### Inferences
- **Arithmetic for this subject (my calculation, not a cited figure):** at 134 kg and ~32% body fat, fat-free mass ≈ 91 kg. The three denominators then give: 2.3 g/kg FFM ≈ **209 g/day** (3.1 g/kg FFM ≈ 282 g, impractical); 1.6 g/kg total mass ≈ **214 g/day**; 2.0 g/kg of target weight (105 kg) ≈ **210 g/day**. The convergence at **~200–230 g/day** is the practical target, and it is reachable. The naive 2.4 g/kg of *total* mass gives 322 g/day, which is both unsupported (no evidence base at that body fat) and unachievable on semaglutide.
- The right app rule is therefore: **compute protein from fat-free mass or from target weight, never from total mass at a physique-literature multiplier.** Using total mass at >30% body fat inflates the target by ~45% because adipose tissue has negligible protein turnover.
- The gap between clinical GLP-1 guidance (1.2–1.6 g/kg actual = 161–214 g at 134 kg) and the physique range is smaller than it looks once the denominator is fixed; the ranges overlap at ~210 g/day. That makes ~2 g/kg FFM / ~1.6 g/kg total / ~200–215 g/day defensible under both literatures.
- Per-meal: at 4 meals (the subject's app caps at 4 meals per the project context), 210 g / 4 = ~52 g per meal. That is above the 0.4 g/kg/meal reference computed on total mass (0.4 × 134 = 54 g) and well above the ISSN 20–40 g and 0.25 g/kg benchmarks, so 4 meals is sufficient; the binding constraint is appetite, not anabolic ceiling.
- Semaglutide changes the protein problem from "how high can we push it" to "how do we hit a floor." With intake down 22–35% and real-world GLP-1 users averaging 77 g/day, the app's protein logic should be built around a **non-negotiable floor (~1.6 g/kg total ≈ 210 g, with ~160 g as a hard minimum)** delivered in protein-dense, low-volume forms, with protein placed first in each meal. Whey/shakes and lean meats become mechanically necessary rather than optional.
- Because GLP-1 users fall short on vitamin D, iron, calcium, magnesium, potassium and fibre as well as protein, and because dehydration is the leading serious adverse event, a micronutrient/fibre/fluid check belongs in the same rule set as the protein floor.

### Gaps
- No RCT has tested a protein dose-response during GLP-1 therapy; all protein targets for this population are extrapolated from non-GLP-1 weight-loss studies (explicitly acknowledged by the reviews).
- No leucine-threshold study exists in people with obesity during a deficit; the 700–3000 mg (ISSN) and 2.5–3.0 g/meal (Arslan) figures come from lean/mixed populations.
- The exact Refalo et al. meta-regression coefficient (change in FFM per g/kg FFM) and its confidence interval were not retrieved — only the >97% probability-of-linearity statement.
- No study establishes whether "target weight" is a valid denominator; it is a practitioner heuristic, not a validated basis.

## Q4. Metabolic adaptation: how much is adaptive vs predicted by mass loss, and how fast does it reverse?

### Takeaway
The adaptive (beyond-mass-predicted) component during active dieting is roughly **100–180 kcal/day**, it roughly halves or disappears once weight is stabilised, and the much larger multi-hundred-kcal figures come from extreme-loss cohorts and are contested. Practically: **recalculate calories from measured weight trend, not from an adaptation correction factor** — the adaptation is smaller than the error in any TDEE formula.

### Cited Findings
- Early-phase adaptation: the drop in energy expenditure averaged about **−178 kcal/day after one week of restriction** and stayed fairly consistent within individuals — [PMC7484122](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7484122)
- During a 1,000 kcal/day diet, measured resting metabolic rate ran about **107 kcal/day below predicted at week 9**; the adaptation **roughly halved after a weight-stabilisation period and was not present at one-year follow-up** — paper titled "Metabolic adaptation is an illusion, only present when participants are in negative energy balance" — [PubMed 32844188](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32844188/)
- At stable weight after a 12 kg loss, adaptation was only about **50 kcal/day** — [PMC7657334](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7657334)
- Dose-response to outcome: greater metabolic adaptation predicted less weight and fat loss on low-energy diets, with **each extra 50 kcal/day of adaptation corresponding to roughly 0.5 kg less loss** — [PubMed 34116675](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34116675/)
- Contested long-term persistence: longitudinal data from very large losses report about **275 kcal/day after a 58 kg loss and about 499 kcal/day at six-year follow-up despite substantial regain**; the same review notes critics argue the effect is exaggerated when weight-stable formerly-obese people are compared with BMI-matched controls — [PMC7657332](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7657332)
- Other analyses found **no link between metabolic adaptation and weight regain** — [PubMed 32844188](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32844188/)
- MATADOR quantified the adaptive component directly under two schedules: adjusted for fat and fat-free mass, REE fell **−749 ± 498 kJ/d (~9%) on continuous restriction vs −360 ± 502 kJ/d (~4%) on intermittent (P = 0.03)**, while the **unadjusted** change did not differ (P = 0.48) — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Hall's modified Forbes model: the energy density of lost tissue depends on initial body fat; the ~3,500 kcal/lb rule of thumb approximately matches obese subjects with **initial fat mass above 30 kg** but overestimates the cumulative deficit required per unit weight loss in leaner people — [Hall 2008, PubMed 17848938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/) / [PMC2376744](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376744)

### Inferences
- MATADOR's −749 vs −360 kJ/d translates to roughly **−179 vs −86 kcal/day** of adaptive REE suppression, i.e. the full-continuous figure matches the ~178 kcal/day early-restriction number from an independent dataset. Converging evidence puts the adaptive component of a 33% deficit at roughly **80–180 kcal/day**, or about **4–9% of REE**.
- "Reversal is fast" is the better-supported position: halving after a stabilisation period and absence at one year (Martins) plus ~50 kcal/day at stable weight after 12 kg. This directly supports the diet-break mechanism *in principle* — but the quantity recovered (tens of kcal/day) is too small to be worth a scheduled break on metabolic grounds alone.
- Practical app rule: TDEE must be **re-derived empirically** on a rolling basis (observed trend-weight change × an energy-density constant, reconciled against logged intake), with recalculation triggered by observed stall rather than by a predicted-adaptation adjustment. A formula-based recalculation that merely re-enters the new lower body weight will under-predict by roughly the adaptive component (~100–180 kcal/day) during active dieting and over-correct after a break.
- Because Hall's model indicates ~3,500 kcal/lb is approximately correct for people with initial fat mass above 30 kg, and the subject's fat mass is ~43 kg, the standard energy-density constant is appropriate for him — this makes an empirical trend-based TDEE recalculation more reliable for him than it would be for a lean athlete.
- The 275–499 kcal/day figures should not be used in app copy for this subject: they come from extreme-loss cohorts (58 kg losses, competition-style protocols) and are the contested end of the literature.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures how fast adaptation reverses during a *1-week* break specifically; the Peos 2021 +50 kcal/day REE change is the closest datum and lacks a control group.
- The amount of the measured REE drop attributable to reduced sympathetic/thyroid activity vs measurement error vs changes in organ mass is not resolved in the sources found.
- Non-resting components (NEAT, thermic effect of food, activity energy expenditure) were not measured in MATADOR and are plausibly larger than the REE component, but I found no quantification for this population.
- No data on metabolic adaptation under GLP-1 therapy specifically.

## Q5. Rate of loss: what weekly rate (% of body mass) preserves lean mass, and when is faster than 1%/week supported?

### Takeaway
The only randomised comparison of loss rates (Garthe, 0.7% vs 1.4%/week in elite athletes training 4×/week) favoured the slow rate for lean mass (**+2.1% vs −0.2%, P < 0.01**) but found **no between-group difference at 12-month follow-up**; nothing in the literature found supports >1%/week in trained lifters, though the Forbes/Hall model predicts a person with >30 kg of fat mass loses proportionally less lean tissue per kg than a lean person — which is the mechanistic case for tolerating a higher rate at high body fat.

### Cited Findings
- Garthe et al.: elite athletes randomised to **0.7%/week (slow) vs 1.4%/week (fast)** body-weight loss, both strength training 4×/week. **Lean body mass increased 2.1% ± 0.4% in slow (P < 0.001) and was unchanged in fast (−0.2% ± 0.7%), between-group P < 0.01.** Mean intervention durations were **8.5 ± 2.2 weeks (slow) vs 5.3 ± 0.9 weeks (fast)** — [Garthe et al. (UiO DUO)](https://duo.uio.no/handle/10852/28711)
- Koivisto/related 2009 trial found the same pattern: lean body mass increased only in the SLOW group, which also achieved a larger fat-mass reduction than the FAST group — [as cited in the athlete body-composition literature](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jorunn-Sundgot-Borgen/publication/51057901_Elite_athletes_in_aesthetic_and_Olympic_weight-class_sports_and_the_challenge_of_body_weight_and_body_composition/links/54b142e40cf2318f0f92542f/Elite-athletes-in-aesthetic-and-Olympic-weight-class-sports-and-the-challenge-of-body-weight-and-body-composition.pdf)
- Long-term follow-up (Garthe et al. 2011, 6 and 12 months post-diet): body mass fell about 6% in both groups, and **after 12 months there were no significant differences between groups in body composition or performance**; the authors concluded **weight-loss rate is not the most important factor in maintaining body composition and performance after weight loss in elite athletes** — [Garthe et al. 2011](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122535/records/65df7eb30f3e94b9e5d93233)
- Practical summary of that literature: athletes who want to gain lean body mass and increase 1RM strength during a weight-loss period combined with strength training should aim for **~0.7% body weight per week** — [suppversity summary of Garthe](https://suppversity.blogspot.com/2011/05/optimal-weight-loss-for-athletes-lose.html) (secondary source)
- ICECAP prescribed **~0.7% of body weight per week** in resistance-trained athletes, with 2.3 g/kg protein, and achieved ~4.2 kg over 12 restricted weeks — [Peos et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362/)
- Deficit-size dose-response (meta-analysis): as the energy deficit grew by 100 kcal/day, the lean-mass effect size tended to drop by **0.031 units**, and a deficit of **~500 kcal/day was predicted to fully blunt lean mass gains** — [as reported by Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/)
- Counter-critique of that same dataset: among deficit-only groups, **there is not a clear relationship between the size of the deficit and the amount of lean mass lost** — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/)
- Starting body fat matters mechanistically: Hall's modification of the Forbes equation makes the lean-mass share of weight loss a nonlinear function of initial body fat and the magnitude of the loss, with **leaner people losing proportionally more lean mass**; the prediction was supported by published weight-loss data from obese and lean subjects — [Hall 2008, PMC2376744](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376744) / [PubMed 17848938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/)
- MATADOR's 33% deficit in men with obesity produced fat-free mass losses of only **1.2–1.8 kg against 9.2–14.1 kg of weight loss** (fat was ~87% of weight lost) on just **15–20% protein** and no resistance training — [MATADOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575/)
- Semaglutide comparison point: in the STEP 1 DEXA substudy (140 participants), total lean body mass **fell about 9.7% from baseline** on semaglutide 2.4 mg at week 68 while its share of total body mass rose ~3 percentage points; total fat mass fell 19.3% and visceral fat 27.4% — [STEP 1 DEXA substudy, PMC8089287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8089287)
- Lean mass as a share of weight lost on semaglutide was **about 40%** (Wilding et al. 2021), with a SUSTAIN 8 substudy showing a nearly identical share at lower doses; across 17 studies of incretin therapy, lean body mass losses of **20–50%** of weight lost were reported, similar to other weight-loss therapies. Tirzepatide: ~6 kg of fat-free mass over 12–18 months, roughly 10% of fat-free mass. Some recent reports suggest incretin therapy does not accelerate skeletal muscle loss specifically, and that part of the extra fat-free mass loss may come from organs such as liver and bone — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies); share-of-loss estimate also at [Attia commentary on the STEP 1 substudy](https://peterattiamd.com/?p=20325)

### Inferences
- **No direct evidence supports >1%/week in trained lifters.** The one randomised test of a faster rate (1.4%/week) cost lean mass during the diet. The mechanistic case for a person at >30% body fat going faster rests on the Forbes/Hall curve and on MATADOR's observation that a 33% deficit cost only ~1.2–1.8 kg of FFM in men with obesity — indirect, but it is real evidence that the Garthe result (lean athletes) does not transfer unmodified.
- **Arithmetic for this subject (my calculation):** 1%/week at 134 kg = 1.34 kg/week; 0.7%/week = ~0.94 kg/week. Going 134 → 105 kg (29 kg) takes roughly **22 weeks at 1%/week declining with body mass, or ~31 weeks at 0.7%/week**. The 0.7–1.0%/week band is where the evidence lives, and the upper end of that band is defensible given his body fat — but encoding anything above 1%/week would be extrapolation.
- A defensible app rule is a **body-fat-scaled rate cap**: ~1.0–1.2%/week allowed while body fat is above ~30%, tapering toward 0.7%/week and then 0.5%/week as he approaches ~105 kg and leans out. This matches the direction of the Forbes/Hall prediction and of the Helms protein recommendation (which also scales with leanness).
- The Garthe 12-month null is important for app messaging: the lean-mass advantage of slow dieting was **not durable** at one year. Rate of loss should be framed as affecting the quality of the diet phase, not the final outcome, which reduces the case for guilt-driven slow dieting.
- **Semaglutide is the dominant risk factor for lean mass here, not the rate of loss.** At ~40% of weight lost coming from fat-free mass without resistance training and adequate protein, a 29 kg loss would imply ~11.6 kg of FFM lost. Against that baseline, the levers worth encoding are resistance training (the GSSI review's most consistently backed lever) and the protein floor — not a slower rate per se. Note that the 20–50% share range is reported as similar to other weight-loss therapies, so semaglutide is probably not uniquely catabolic; the problem is that it suppresses the protein intake needed to defend against normal deficit-driven FFM loss.
- Because he lifts 5–6 days/week and is on a drug that cuts intake 22–35%, the plausible failure mode is an **unintentionally excessive deficit** (drug-driven under-eating) rather than too slow a rate. The app should treat a weekly loss rate persistently above ~1.2%/week as an alert condition requiring a calorie increase, not as success.

### Gaps
- No randomised comparison of loss rates has been run in people with obesity who resistance train, and none in anyone on GLP-1 therapy. The 0.7% vs 1.4% comparison is elite athletes only, n ≈ 23–30, and the reported lean-mass figures differ slightly between versions of the data (2.1% in one abstract, 2.0% vs 0.8% in the long-term report).
- The GSSI review states explicitly that **no clinical trials have tested resistance training outcomes with incretin therapy**, and that the optimal mode, frequency and intensity are unknown.
- Public-health activity guidance on GLP-1 therapy is generic: 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous activity with **at least 2 days/week of whole-body resistance training** (Jakicic et al. 2024); whether higher volumes (200–300 min/week) help on these medications is explicitly described as unclear — [GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/nutritional-and-physical-activity-optimization-for-glp-1-based-therapies). There is no evidence base for or against the subject's 5–6 d/week frequency in this context.
- The exact meta-analysis behind the "−0.031 effect size per 100 kcal/day" figure was reached only through a secondary source (Stronger By Science); the primary paper was not retrieved and the figure should be verified before use.
- No source found quantifies how much a refeed or diet break changes lean-mass retention *on* a GLP-1 drug; all such rules in an app would be mechanistic extrapolation and must be labelled as such.
